<i><b>A Tale of the House of the Wolfings and All the Kindreds of the Mark</b></i> is a novel by [William Morris](/wiki/William_Morris). It was the first of the author's 'prose romances', written during his last eight years. The novel was followed by *[The Roots of the Mountains](/wiki/The_Roots_of_the_Mountains)*.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

In this novel Morris was perhaps the first to have used the English word *[Mirkwood](/wiki/Mirkwood)*, likely derived from [Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse). In the same manner, [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) had created *Mirkwood*, although it can be seen in [a letter to his grandson](</wiki/Letter_to_Michael_George_Tolkien_(29_July_1966)> "Letter to Michael George Tolkien (29 July 1966)") that he derived the word independently of Morris. Tolkien had, however, bought *The House of the Wolfings* as early as in spring [1914](/wiki/1914),<sup id="cite_ref-CGMorris_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGMorris-2">[2]</a></sup> and commented in a 1960 letter that he was inspired by *The House of the Wolfings* in his depiction of the [Dead Marshes](/wiki/Dead_Marshes) and the approaches to the [Morannon](/wiki/Morannon) in *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)*.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>

Tolkien scholars have discussed several other possible instances of influence that *The House of the Wolfings* may have had on Tolkien: both specific ones such as the [Red Arrow](/wiki/Red_Arrow#Inspiration) and *Mid-mark* (compare *[Mark](/wiki/Riddermark "Riddermark")*), and a more general influence such as "*the Germanic sense of enemy and battle*"<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> and a literary style "*heavily laden with archaisms and poetic inversions in an attempt to recreate the aura of ancient legend*".<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>
