<i><b>The Fall of Arthur</b></i> is an unfinished poem by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) that is concerned with the legend of <a class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" href="/wiki/King_Arthur" title="King Arthur">King Arthur</a>. It was published in [2013](/wiki/2013), together with three essays by [Christopher Tolkien](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien).

The poem is in an alliterative form, extending to nearly 1,000 verses imitating the Old English *[Beowulf](</wiki/Beowulf_(poem)> "Beowulf (poem)")* metre in Modern English, and inspired by high medieval Arthurian fiction.

## Background {#Background}

The poem's existence was first revealed in [1981](/wiki/1977 "1977") when *[The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien)* was published. In [a 1955 letter to Houghton Mifflin](/wiki/Letter_165 "Letter 165"), Tolkien, discussing his use of alliterative verse, mentioned that he hoped to finish his "long poem" *The Fall of Arthur*.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

In his [1977 biography of Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien:_A_Biography "J.R.R. Tolkien: A Biography"), [Humphrey Carpenter](/wiki/Humphrey_Carpenter) published a few brief extracts of the poem and commented that it "has alliteration but no rhyme \[and\] did not touch on the Grail but began an individual rendering of the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Morte_d%27Arthur" title="wikipedia:Le Morte d&#39;Arthur">Morte d&#39;Arthur</a>, in which the king and Gawain go to war in 'Saxon lands' but are summoned home by news of Mordred's treachery".<sup id="cite_ref-Bio_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio-2">[2]</a></sup> It was also revealed that "The Fall of Arthur" was read and approved by both [E.V. Gordon](/wiki/E.V._Gordon) and [R.W. Chambers](/wiki/R.W._Chambers),<sup id="cite_ref-Bio_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup> and that the writing of the poem was abandoned in the mid 1930s.<sup id="cite_ref-Bio_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio-2">[2]</a></sup>

## From the publisher {#From_the_publisher}

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>The world first publication of a previously unknown work by <a href="/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien" title="J.R.R. Tolkien">J.R.R. Tolkien</a>, which tells the extraordinary story of the final days of England’s legendary hero, King Arthur.
</p><p><i>The Fall of Arthur</i>, the only venture by J.R.R. Tolkien into the legends of Arthur King of Britain, may well be regarded as his finest and most skilful achievement in the use of the <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a> alliterative metre, in which he brought to his transforming perceptions of the old narratives a pervasive sense of the grave and fateful nature of all that is told: of Arthur’s expedition overseas into distant heathen lands, of Guinevere’s flight from Camelot, of the great sea-battle on Arthur’s return to Britain, in the portrait of the traitor Mordred, in the tormented doubts of Lancelot in his French castle.
</p><p>Unhappily, <i>The Fall of Arthur</i> was one of several long narrative poems that he abandoned in that period. In this case he evidently began it in the earlier nineteen-thirties, and it was sufficiently advanced for him to send it to a very perceptive friend who read it with great enthusiasm at the end of <a href="/wiki/1934" title="1934">1934</a> and urgently pressed him ‘You simply must finish it!’ But in vain: he abandoned it, at some date unknown, though there is some evidence that it may have been in 1937, the year of the publication of <i><a href="/wiki/The_Hobbit" title="The Hobbit">The Hobbit</a></i> and the first stirrings of <i><a href="/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings" title="The Lord of the Rings">The Lord of the Rings</a></i>. Years later, in a <a href="/wiki/Letter_165" title="Letter 165">letter of 1955</a>, he said that ‘he hoped to finish a long poem on <i>The Fall of Arthur</i>’; but that day never came.
</p><p>Associated with the text of the poem, however, are many manuscript pages: a great quantity of drafting and experimentation in verse, in which the strange evolution of the poem’s structure is revealed, together with narrative synopses and very significant if tantalising notes. In these latter can be discerned clear if mysterious associations of the Arthurian conclusion with <i><a href="/wiki/The_Silmarillion" title="The Silmarillion">The Silmarillion</a></i>, and the bitter ending of the love of Lancelot and Guinevere, which was never written.
</p>
</blockquote>

## Errata {#Errata}

There are some typos in the book:

- According to Nelson Goering at Tolkien Society facebook group: "at line 182 we learn that 'hosemen hastened'. Now I'm wondering just what the role of the hoseman was. Perhaps it's a kind of squire, who travels with his knight to ensure he always has clean, dry, well-pressed trousers. Or perhaps it is something nobler, a high chivalric order marked out by their elegant legwear".<sup id="cite_ref-Facebook_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Facebook-6">[6]</a></sup>

- Nelson Goering also said "Canto II, line 76 is mislineated. It says 'He hastens home, and his / host summons', but should be 'He hastens home, / and his host summons'".<sup id="cite_ref-Facebook_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Facebook-6">[6]</a></sup>
