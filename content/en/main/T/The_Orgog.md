<i><b>The Orgog</b></i> is the title of an unfinished and unpublished<sup id="cite_ref-rg_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rg-1">[1]</a></sup> children's tale written by [J.R.R. Tolkien](../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md) "around [1924](../_/1924.md)" about "a strange creature traveling through a fantastic landscape".<sup id="cite_ref-firstmention_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-firstmention-2">[2]</a></sup>

## Background {#Background}

When the Tolkien family had moved in [1924](../_/1924.md) to 2 Darnley Road in Leeds,<sup id="cite_ref-AI_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-3">[3]</a></sup> [John Tolkien](../J/John_Tolkien.md) recalls that *The Orgog* was one of the tales read by his father to him, when he could not fall asleep.<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-4">[4]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-rg_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rg-1">[1]</a></sup> While most of these tales were not written down,<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-4">[4]</a></sup> *The Orgog* survives as an unfinished, typewritten manuscript.<sup id="cite_ref-AI_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-3">[3]</a></sup> Not much is known of the story itself, except that it is a "strange, convoluted tale of an odd creature travelling through a fantastic landscape"<sup id="cite_ref-AI_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-3">[3]</a></sup> and includes "blue mountains and an orange sun"<sup id="cite_ref-AI_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-3">[3]</a></sup>.<sup id="cite_ref-firstmention_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-firstmention-2">[2]</a></sup>

It has been suggested by [Wayne G. Hammond](../W/Wayne_G._Hammond.md) and [Christina Scull](../C/Christina_Scull.md) that Tolkien's watercolour *A Shop on the Edge of the Hills of Fairy Land* (painted in 1924) is likely related to the tale,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[note 1]</a></sup> as the word "Gogs" appears on the depicted shop building, though there is no mention of such a shop in the tale. However, like *The Orgog*, it also has "blue mountains and an orange sun". The painting is dedicated to John. In September [1936](../_/1936.md), [Christopher Tolkien](../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md) accidentally tore it up, though Tolkien repaired it.<sup id="cite_ref-AI_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-3">[3]</a></sup>

On [22 June](../_/22_June.md) [2025](../_/2025.md), [Jason Fisher](../J/Jason_Fisher.md) mentioned on his blog that he had been told that *The Orgog* "really isn’t in a state suitable for publication" in a private correspondence.<sup id="cite_ref-lingwe_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lingwe-6">[5]</a></sup>

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The meaning of "orgog", and if the word has any connection to one of Tolkien's invented [languages](../../category/L/Languages.md), is unknown. It was speculated by Jason Fisher on his blog that the word may

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>relate to “<a href="../O/Ogres.md" title="Ogres">ogre</a>”, perhaps connected with <a href="../H/Hilary_Tolkien.md" title="Hilary Tolkien">Hilary Tolkien</a>’s (and Ronald’s) childhood tales of black and white ogres. Or there is the character of Orgoglio from Edmund Spenser’s <i>The Faerie Queene</i>. That work was more to Lewis’s taste than Tolkien’s, but you never know. In that work, Orgoglio is a monster who attacks Redcross Knight in Book One, Canto VII. The name, interestingly enough, is Italian for “pride”, from Vulgar Latin *<i>orgollium</i>, in turn from Proto-West Germanic *<i>urgollju</i> “pride, arrogance”.<sup id="cite_ref-lingwe_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lingwe-6">[5]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

## Inspiration {#Inspiration}

Although it might be a mere coincidence, the name *Orgog* is reminiscent of <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orgoglio" title="wikipedia:Orgoglio">Orgoglio</a>, the name of a giant appearing in Book I of <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser" title="wikipedia:Edmund Spenser">Edmund Spenser&#39;s</a> <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Faerie_Queene" title="wikipedia:The Faerie Queene">The Faerie Queene</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[note 2]</a></sup>
