**Sir Terence David John Pratchett**, [CBE](/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire "Order of the British Empire") ([28 April](/wiki/28_April) [1948](/wiki/1948) - [12 March](/wiki/12_March) [2015](/wiki/2015)), more commonly just simply known as **Terry Pratchett**, was an English author who primarily wrote fantasy fiction. His comic fantasy book series *Discworld* frequently parodies, or takes inspiration from, [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien).

On [22 November](/wiki/22_November) [1967](/wiki/1967), before the start of his writer's career, Pratchett sent a letter to Tolkien, where he praised *[Smith of Wootton Major](/wiki/Smith_of_Wootton_Major)* and expressed his hope to see *[The Silmarillion](/wiki/The_Silmarillion)* in the near future, later receiving a warm reply.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>

## Quotes {#Quotes}

In an interview with The New York Times, Pratchett was asked “Who are your favorite fantasy novelists?” to which he replied:

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>O.K., I give in. J. R. R. Tolkien. I wrote a letter to him once and got a very nice reply. Just think how busy he would have been, and yet he took the time out to write to a fan.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r395759">.mw-parser-output .tg-quote-container{margin:1rem 0}.mw-parser-output .tg-quote-container>.tg-section-divider{margin:0}.mw-parser-output .tg-quote-container>.tg-section-divider>span{line-height:0;font-size:2rem;margin-top:1rem}.mw-parser-output .tg-quote-container>.tg-quote{padding-left:0;border-left:none}</style>

In another interview Pratchett was asked: "Why do you feel uncomfortable with being ahead of Tolkien?" and he replied:

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>I think Tolkien will be around in a hundred years time but I’m not certain that I will.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>[W]hen I was young I wrote a letter to J.R.R. Tolkien, just as he was becoming extravagantly famous. I think the book that impressed me was Smith of Wootton Major. Mine must have been among hundreds or thousands of letters he received every week. I got a reply. It might have been dictated. For all I know, it might have been typed to a format. But it was signed. He must have had a sackful of letters from every commune and university in the world, written by people whose children are now grown-up and trying to make a normal life while being named Galadriel or Moonchild. It wasn’t as if I’d said a lot. There were no numbered questions. I just said that I’d enjoyed the book very much. And he said thank you. For a moment, it achieved the most basic and treasured of human communications: you are real, and therefore so am I.<sup id="cite_ref-Book_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Book-5">[5]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

He also talked about how influential Tolkien has been in modern fantasy in his essay 'Magic Kingdoms':

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>J.R.R. Tolkien has become a sort of mountain, appearing in all subsequent fantasy in the way that Mt. Fuji appears so often in Japanese prints. Sometimes it’s big and up close. Sometimes it’s a shape on the horizon. Sometimes it’s not there at all, which means that the artist either has made a deliberate decision against the mountain, which is interesting in itself, or is in fact standing on Mt. Fuji.<sup id="cite_ref-Book_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Book-5">[5]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>
