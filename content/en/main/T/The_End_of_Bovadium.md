<i><b>The End of Bovadium</b></i> (author's title)<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup> or <i><b>The Bovadium Fragments</b></i> is a short story by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) written between mid 1957<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> and early 1960.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> According to [Richard Ovenden](/wiki/Richard_Ovenden), the [Bodleian Library](/wiki/Bodleian_Library)'s librarian, *The Bovadium Fragments* "is about a scholar in the future looking at evidence of a society that is now lost" due to worshipping automobiles.<sup id="cite_ref-telegraph_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-telegraph-5">[5]</a></sup> [John Garth](/wiki/John_Garth) describes *The Bovadium Fragments* as "a satire on modernity, without adventures", taking place in "a post-apocalyptic future when civilisation as we know it has collapsed" because of cars.<sup id="cite_ref-road_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-road-6">[6]</a></sup>

## Synopsis {#Synopsis}

### Foreword {#Foreword}

The story begins with a Foreword by [Doctor Sarevelk](/wiki/Doctor_Sarevelk) discussing "[a site](/wiki/Bovadium "Bovadium") of historical"<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> importance "found at *[Vasti](/wiki/V%C3%A2sti "Vâsti")*"<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> "on the borders of the [Southern Region](/wiki/Southern_Region)",<sup id="cite_ref-Foreword_7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foreword-7">[7]</a></sup> and two" "equally dead and obscure"<sup id="cite_ref-road_6-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-road-6">[6]</a></sup> languages that are referred to as "*[A](/wiki/Latin "Latin")* and *[B](/wiki/Modern_English "Modern English")*".<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> While Language B has been "almost entirely deciphered" at the time of the story using "the [Rotzopny](/wiki/Rotzopny)–[Dwarf](/wiki/Dwarf) treatment", language A is not. In the second part of the Foreword, [Dr. Gums](/wiki/Dr._Gums) summarizes the three fragments of the story proper and explains his method of translating them in a smug way.<sup id="cite_ref-Foreword_7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foreword-7">[7]</a></sup>

### Fragment I {#Fragment_I}

The first of the three fragments discussed is "a chronicle or legend in language A"<sup id="cite_ref-Foreword_7-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foreword-7">[7]</a></sup> which Fragment II, according to Dr. Gums,<sup id="cite_ref-Foreword_7-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foreword-7">[7]</a></sup> is a translation of.<sup id="cite_ref-FI_8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FI-8">[8]</a></sup>

### Fragment II {#Fragment_II}

The second of the three fragments discussed is a translation and expanded version of Fragment I,<sup id="cite_ref-FII_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FII-9">[9]</a></sup> describing the takeover of Bovadium by the Motores.<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup>

### Fragment III {#Fragment_III}

The third of the three fragments discussed is a text in Language B with inserts from language A that describes the aftermath of the end of Bovadium.<sup id="cite_ref-FIII_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FIII-10">[10]</a></sup>

### Postscript by the Editor {#Postscript_by_the_Editor}

The story concludes with a fictitious Postscript by Dr. Gums.<sup id="cite_ref-Postscript_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Postscript-11">[11]</a></sup>

## Other Texts of Fragment II {#Other_Texts_of_Fragment_II}

Following the story, [Christopher Tolkien](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien) describes two additional texts of Fragment II both entitled *[Domine defende nos contra hos Motores bos!](/wiki/Domine_defende_nos_contra_hos_Motores_bos!)*. The first is a poem "in thirty-one octosyllabic couplets"<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> while the second is a Latin text which Christopher Tolkien translated.<sup id="cite_ref-Other_12-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Other-12">[12]</a></sup>

## Background {#Background}

Sometime between mid [1957](/wiki/1957)<sup id="cite_ref-guidereview_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-guidereview-3">[3]</a></sup> and early [1960](/wiki/1960),<sup id="cite_ref-october_13-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-october-13">[13]</a></sup> Tolkien wrote a short<sup id="cite_ref-october_13-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-october-13">[13]</a></sup> "satire"<sup id="cite_ref-satire_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-satire-14">[14]</a></sup> entitled *The End of Bovadium*<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup> or *The Bovadium Fragments*.<sup id="cite_ref-Bio_15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio-15">[15]</a></sup> Tolkien initially wrote it "for his own amusement" as "a private academic jest" to gently satirize "pomposities of archaeologists" and the "hideousness of college crockery", being inspired by <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Denis_Godley" title="wikipedia:Alfred Denis Godley">A.D. Godley</a>'s <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Motor_Bus" title="wikipedia:The Motor Bus">Motor Bus</a></i> comic poem and a "<a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=The_Origin_of_Bovadium&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="The Origin of Bovadium (page does not exist)">recrudescence of the debate</a> about [Oxford](/wiki/Oxford) roads".<sup id="cite_ref-PN_16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PN-16">[16]</a></sup>

On [25 October](/wiki/25_October) [1960](/wiki/1960), Tolkien sought (through his secretary <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Elisabeth_Lumsden&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Elisabeth Lumsden (page does not exist)">Elisabeth Lumsden</a>) to publish what is believed to be this writing (since it was described as "a sort of satirical fantasy") in the magazine <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_and_Tide_(magazine)" title="wikipedia:Time and Tide (magazine)">Time and Tide</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">[note 1]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-october_13-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-october-13">[13]</a></sup> In a [letter](/wiki/Letter_231a "Letter 231a") to <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Joan_Anne_Tolkien&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Joan Anne Tolkien (page does not exist)">Joan Anne Tolkien</a> in [November](/wiki/November) [1961](/wiki/1961), Tolkien mentioned the suffering of Oxford "from the ravages of the machine-worshippers".<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">[17]</a></sup> On [24 August](/wiki/24_August) [1966](/wiki/1966), Tolkien lent [Rayner Unwin](/wiki/Rayner_Unwin) the manuscript,<sup id="cite_ref-august_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-august-19">[18]</a></sup> who wrote to Tolkien already the next day, thinking that he "should publish it in the <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Oxford_Magazine&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Oxford Magazine (page does not exist)">Oxford Magazine</a>".<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">[19]</a></sup> Later, around the same time,<sup id="cite_ref-PN_16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-PN-16">[16]</a></sup> [Clyde S. Kilby](/wiki/Clyde_S._Kilby) was also given a copy, being asked if the piece was worthy of publication.<sup id="cite_ref-satire_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-satire-14">[14]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-august_19-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-august-19">[18]</a></sup>

On or before [29 November](/wiki/29_November) [1968](/wiki/1968), *The End of Bovadium*<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup> was included in a list of unpublished works that Tolkien had his secretary, [Joy Hill](/wiki/Joy_Hill), compile.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">[20]</a></sup> In a letter to Rayner Unwin on [2 December](/wiki/2_December), Tolkien commented that, though *The End of Bovadium* was on the list of unpublished works, he had "no intention of publishing" it to keep it from preventing him from working on his "[proper work](/wiki/The_Silmarillion "The Silmarillion")".<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup>

After Tolkien's death in [1973](/wiki/1973), Clyde S. Kilby would later recall the story in April [1976](/wiki/1976):

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>Though the reading of <i>The Silmarillon</i> was proving about as much as I could handle during that summer of 1966, Tolkien from time to time handed me other shorter pieces and asked me about their publishability. One was called “The Bovadium Fragments,” a satire written long before and having as its main point the worship of the <i>Motores</i>, i.e., automobiles, and the traffic jams blocking the roads in and around Oxford. It was full of the inventiveness to be expected of Tolkien. Some of the characters are <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Rotzopny" title="Rotzopny">Rotzopny</a>, <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Dr._Gums" title="Dr. Gums">Dr. Gums</a>, and <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Doctor_Sarevelk" title="Doctor Sarevelk">Sarevelk</a>. I judged that it had two elements that would make it unpublishable. One was the more than liberal use of Latin, and the other the probability that a reader&#39;s eye would focus on its playfulness rather than its serious implications. Actually it was an early comment on the commercialization of our world.<sup id="cite_ref-satire_14-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-satire-14">[14]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

A few more details about the contents were provided in [Humphrey Carpenter](/wiki/Humphrey_Carpenter)'s summary dating from [1977](/wiki/1977), saying that:

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>‘The Bovadium Fragments’ (perhaps composed early in the nineteen-sixties) is a parable of the destruction of <a href="/wiki/Oxford" title="Oxford">Oxford</a> (<i>Bovadium</i>) by the <i>motores</i> manufactured by the <i><a href="/wiki/Daemon" title="Daemon">Daemon</a></i> of <i><a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Vaccipratum" title="Vaccipratum">Vaccipratum</a></i> (a reference to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Nuffield" title="wikipedia:Lord Nuffield">Lord Nuffield</a> and his motor-works at Cowley) which blocks the streets, asphyxiate the inhabitants, and finally explode.<sup id="cite_ref-Bio_15-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio-15">[15]</a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

On [27 June](/wiki/27_June), [Shaun Gunner](/wiki/Shaun_Gunner) noted in an article on the website of [The Tolkien Society](/wiki/The_Tolkien_Society) that *The End of Bovadium*<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup> is "Tolkien’s comment on industrialisation and environmental concerns" and is similar but different compared to *Mr. Bliss*.<sup id="cite_ref-society_37-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-society-37">[36]</a></sup> On [21 July](/wiki/21_July), Nancy Steinman stated in an article on [TheOneRing.net](/wiki/TheOneRing.net) that *The End of Bovadium*<sup id="cite_ref-CGL_2-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-CGL-2">[2]</a></sup> reflected "Tolkien’s disdain of industrialism", comparing the story to "[The Scouring of the Shire](/wiki/The_Scouring_of_the_Shire)" chapter in *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)*.<sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">[37]</a></sup> On [3 October](/wiki/3_October), Sam Leith wrote a harsh criticism of the 2025 book on the website of "The Telegraph".<sup id="cite_ref-criticism_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-criticism-1">[1]</a></sup> In another review, John Garth described "the vision of dead motorists reaching the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styx" title="wikipedia:Styx">River Styx</a> and encountering the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stygian" title="wikipedia:Stygian">stygian</a> ferryman <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charon" title="wikipedia:Charon">Charon</a>" with a "motor boat" was "particularly enjoyable".<sup id="cite_ref-road_6-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-road-6">[6]</a></sup>
