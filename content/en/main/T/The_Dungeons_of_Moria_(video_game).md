<i><b>The Dungeons of Moria</b></i>, also known as *Moria*, is a <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue-like" title="wikipedia:Rogue-like">rogue-like</a> computer game inspired by *[The Lord of the Rings](The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md)* and *[Dungeons and Dragons](../D/Dungeons_and_Dragons.md)*. The goal of the game is to reach the bottom of the maze of mines of [Moria](../M/Moria.md) and kill the [Balrog](../B/Balrogs.md).<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>

## Development {#Development}

Robert Alan Koeneke started working on *The Dungeons of Moria* in [1980](../_/1980.md) or [1981](../_/1981.md) at the University of Oklahoma after he became hooked on the game *Rogue* but could not run it on the VAX 11/780 computer running VMS to which he had access. Hence, he decided to write his own *Rogue* game. The first version - *Moria Beta 1.0* - was written with VMS BASIC and resembled Rogue. In 1983 he converted the game to VMS PASCAL, utilizing its new feature, variable length strings, and improved the game's data structures and optimization. In summer 1983, Koeneke completed *Moria 1.0* and it spread among students of the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma" title="wikipedia:University of Oklahoma">University of Oklahoma</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup> Jimmey Todd, a close friend of Koeneke, rewrote the character generator and added skills, history and several other functions. This became *Moria 2.0*.<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup>

In 1983-1985, Koeneke, heeding player advice, endeavored to improve the game and fix bugs. Every time a player completed the game, he would increase the difficulty of the game, eventually claiming that the game was unbeatable. In light of a football match between the University of Oklahoma and the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas" title="wikipedia:University of Texas">University of Texas</a>, Koeneke sent an edited, unbeatable version of the game to University of Texas students. From around 1985 the source code was widely distributed under a licence that permitted sharing and modification but not commercial use. Koeneke's last release was *Moria 4.7* in [1986](../_/1986.md) or [1987](../_/1987.md).<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup> Koeneke ceased his development when he left university and started working for <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines" title="wikipedia:American Airlines">American Airlines</a>. At the time *Moria 5.0* was still in progress. This update would be a complete rewrite and add several features, including bodies of water with water monsters, mysterious orbs and new weapons and treasures.<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup> Development was continued by his programming assistants, Jimmy Wayne Todd and Gary D. McAdoom.<sup id="cite_ref-Angelfire_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Angelfire-1">[1]</a></sup> It is unknown if this version was ever finished, but according to Koeneke this is unlikely.<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup>

The game was officially maintained by David Joseph Grabiner. The last update was *Moria 5.5.6*, which was released in [1994](../_/1994.md), after which activity seems to have been ceased.<sup id="cite_ref-Tolkien_Games_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Tolkien_Games-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> The game was shared globally and several derivative versions were made. Koeneke received thousands letters from players about the game and their exploits.<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup> In a <a class="external text" href="http://groups.google.com/forum/?hl=en#!msg/rec.games.roguelike.angband/gFiS2tV_-AA/Gp7g-TfuJmUJ" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">forum at Google Groups</a> he said:

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>I have since received thousands of letters from all over the world
from players telling about their exploits, and from administrators
cursing the day I was born...  I received mail from behind the iron
curtain (while it was still standing) talking about the game on VAX&#39;s [<i>sic</i>]
(which supposedly couldn&#39;t be there due to export laws).  I used to
have a map with pins for every letter I received, but I gave up on
that!
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>Robert Alan Koeneke<sup id="cite_ref-Interview_Koeneke_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Interview_Koeneke-4">[4]</a></sup></cite></p>
</blockquote>

## Derivative versions {#Derivative_versions}

*Moria* inspired a number of derivative versions. Jim E. Wilson created *Umoria*, a modified version in C for UNIX. At the University of Washington a modified Pascal version named *Imoria* was developed, which has been ported to C by Steve Kertes. *Angband* was derived from *Umoria* at the University of Warwick. Furthermore, it is known to have been an inspiration for *Diablo*.
