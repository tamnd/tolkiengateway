<i><b>The Lord of the Rings</b></i> is an epic high-fantasy book by [J.R.R. Tolkien](../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md). Set in [Middle-earth](../M/Middle-earth.md), the story began as a sequel to Tolkien's earlier work, *[The Hobbit](The_Hobbit.md)*, but eventually developed into a much larger work. The writing began in [1937](../_/1937.md), and was published in three volumes in [1954](../_/1954.md) and [1955](../_/1955.md). *The Lord of the Rings* is one of the best-selling books ever written, with over 150 million copies sold.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

The [book's title](<../L/Lord_of_the_Ring_(title).md>) refers to the story's main antagonist, the Dark Lord [Sauron](../S/Sauron.md), who in an earlier age created the [One Ring](../O/One_Ring.md) to rule the other [Rings of Power](../R/Rings_of_Power.md) given to [Men](../M/Men.md), [Dwarves](../D/Dwarves.md), and [Elves](../E/Elves.md), in his campaign to conquer all of Middle-earth. From homely beginnings in [the Shire](The_Shire.md), a [hobbit](../H/Hobbit.md) land reminiscent of the English countryside, the story ranges across Middle-earth, following the quest to destroy the One Ring mainly through the eyes of the hobbits [Frodo](../F/Frodo.md), [Sam](../S/Sam.md), [Merry](../M/Merry.md), and [Pippin](../P/Pippin.md).

## Inscriptions {#Inscriptions}

Similarly with *The Hobbit*, where Tolkien drew [runes](../R/Runes.md) on the dust-jacket, there are inscriptions in the title pages of all three volumes. [Cirth](../C/Cirth.md) is used in the upper inscription of the title page, where it reads:

<dl><dd>&#34;<i>The Lord of the Rings translated from the Red Book</i>&#34;...</dd></dl>

The sentence follows in the bottom inscription, written in [Tengwar](Tengwar.md):

<dl><dd>...&#34;<i>of Westmarch by John Ronald Reuel Tolkien. Herein is set forth the history of the War of the Ring and the Return of the King as seen by the hobbits.</i>&#34;</dd></dl>

The Cirth inscription is according to the mode of [Angerthas Erebor](../A/Angerthas_Erebor.md), which Tolkien also used to write English (representing [Westron](../W/Westron.md)) on [Balin's Tomb](../B/Balin's_Tomb.md) and in the [Book of Mazarbul](../B/Book_of_Mazarbul.md).

In [Appendix E](../A/Appendix_E.md) Tolkien says that the "English mode" used in the tengwar inscription is not phonetic, but a improvisation between the phonetic values of the letters in [Westron](../W/Westron.md), and the traditional spelling of English ("what a [man of Gondor](../G/Gondorians.md) might have produced").<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>

Tolkien had made some minor spelling errors, which were printed in the first release of the first two volumes, not corrected until the first print of *The Return of the King*.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. liii</sup>

More recent editions of the book(s) feature the Cirth inscription running along the upper and lower edges of the cover or dust-jacket.

## Maps {#Maps}

Three maps in total have been included since the first edition, these are:

- *[A Part of the Shire](../A/A_Part_of_the_Shire.md)* (included in Volume 1)
- *[Map of Rohan, Gondor, and Mordor](../M/Map_of_Rohan,_Gondor,_and_Mordor.md)* (included in Volume 3)
- *[The West of Middle-earth at the End of the Third Age](The_West_of_Middle-earth_at_the_End_of_the_Third_Age.md)* (included in all three volumes; this map was made in [1980](../_/1980.md) replacing [the 1953 version](../G/General_Map_of_Middle-earth.md))

Since the first edition (and generally in many hardcover editions) the maps are printed in black and red, with the two latter as fold-out sheets.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lv</sup>

The final original maps were drawn by [Christopher Tolkien](../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md) who signed as C.J.R.T. For the [1994](../_/1994.md) revised edition by [HarperCollins](../H/HarperCollins.md), [Stephen Raw](../S/Stephen_Raw.md) was commissioned to redraw the maps in black and white, suitable for the smaller sizes.

## Writing process {#Writing_process}

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>It is written in my life-blood, such as that is, thick or thin; and I can do no other.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>J.R.R. Tolkien to his publisher, <a href="../L/Letter_109.md" title="Letter 109">Letter 109</a> (dated <a href="../_/31_July.md" title="31 July">31 July</a> <a href="../_/1947.md" title="1947">1947</a>).</cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div></div>

Despite the success of *[The Hobbit](The_Hobbit.md)*, Tolkien did not originally intend to write a sequel. He did not think that he had many more things to say about hobbits, while a story exploring [Gandalf](../G/Gandalf.md) and the [Necromancer](../N/Necromancer.md) (requested by a reader) would be "too dark".<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> He instead wrote several other children's stories, including *[Roverandom](../R/Roverandom.md)* and *[Farmer Giles of Ham](../F/Farmer_Giles_of_Ham.md)*. His main focus was expanding the history of [Arda](../A/Arda.md) and the [Silmarils](../S/Silmarils.md), and their languages. He also delivered unfinished drafts of the *[Quenta Silmarillion](<../Q/Quenta_Silmarillion_(Lost_Road).md>)* and *[The Gest of Beren and Lúthien](The_Gest_of_Beren_and_Lúthien.md)* to [Allen and Unwin](../A/Allen_&_Unwin.md), which were rejected, and considered a fleshing-out of *[The Adventures of Tom Bombadil](<The_Adventures_of_Tom_Bombadil_(poem).md>)*.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>

Persuaded by his publishers, Tolkien finally started work on a sequel in December 1937. After several false starts, the story of the One Ring soon emerged, and the book mutated from being a sequel to *The Hobbit* to being, in theme, more of a sequel to the unpublished *[Silmarillion](The_Silmarillion.md)*. The idea of the first chapter (*A Long-Expected Party*) arrived fully-formed, although the reasons behind Bilbo's disappearance, and the significance of the Ring, did not arrive, along with the title *The Lord of the Rings*, until spring [1938](../_/1938.md). Originally he was going to write another story in which Bilbo had used up all his treasure and was looking for another adventure to gain more; however, he remembered the Ring and the powers it had and decided to write about that instead. He started to write it with Bilbo as the main character, but decided that the story was too serious to use the fun loving Hobbit. He thought about using Bilbo's son but this generated some difficult questions — Where was his wife? How could Bilbo let his son go into that kind of danger? — so he looked for an alternate character to carry the ring. In Greek legend, it was a hero's nephew that gained the item of power, and so Frodo came into existence.

Writing was slow due to Tolkien's perfectionism, and was frequently interrupted by his academic obligations and, during the war, Civil Defence.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> After many false starts and revisions, he made his method to making a fair copy of what he already had written each time he proceeded with a long new stretch of the narrative.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> However the five final chapters of Book III were written as a whole (not in consequence) during his spare time in summer and autumn of [1942](../_/1942.md).<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxiv</sup>

Occupied with accuracy and realism, Tolkien also made [time-schemes](The_Chronology_of_The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md): chronological lists and tables to keep track of simultaneous events of the story, which he changed and emended according to the story's development, or rejected; since around the departure from [Lothlórien](../L/Lothlórien.md), the schemes become synoptic, across a series of columns, keeping track of the movements of the separate protagonists as well as the enemies.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xlv</sup> Major revisions to those tables happened as late as while writing Book V, when Tolkien decided that [time](Time.md) passed normally in Lothlórien, and introduced the [Shire Reckoning](../S/Shire_Reckoning.md).<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xlvi, xlvii</sup> Those entries also provide specific times (which don't appear in the narrative) of the rise and setting of the [Sun](../S/Sun.md) and [Moon](../M/Moon.md), and when journeys began or ended, aiding him in deciding what sequences of events plausibly could be fitted into a given period.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xlviii</sup>

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>The writing of <i>The Lord of the Rings</i> is laborious, because I have been doing it as well as I know how, and considering every word
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>J.R.R. Tolkien to C.A. Furth, <a href="../L/Letter_35.md" title="Letter 35">Letter 35</a></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div></div>

He seems to have abandoned the book during most of [1943](../_/1943.md), although, with the assistance of his son, [Christopher](../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md), they worked on other projects, such as *[A Part of the Shire](../A/A_Part_of_the_Shire.md)* map, drawn by Christopher based on his father's four extant maps of [The Shire](The_Shire.md)<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lvi</sup> at least before leaving for [South Africa](../S/South_Africa.md) to serve in the Royal Air Force that July.

Writing re-started in Spring [1944](../_/1944.md). This effort was written as a serial for Christopher, and also read to the [Inklings](../I/Inklings.md), including [C.S. Lewis](../C/C.S._Lewis.md), whose enthusiasm encouraged Tolkien.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxv</sup> In May, reaching the end of Book IV, the story came "to the nubb, when the threads must be gathered and the times synchronized and the narrative interwoven", while the story had grown much larger and deeper than he expected, obsoleting his previous sketches.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup> In the wake of the summer exams, he forced himself to write after-hours in order to reach the story to a suitable cliffhanger, the capture of Frodo, by the end of May.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup> However he was unable to find inspiration during the summer.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">[10]</a></sup> In October he made some failed attempts to start Book V, and struggled with the timeline of the narrative (to the point of not only stopping his progress but also interfering with his urgent duties) and had to make some map alterations, and changes in the text, like adding some extra days;<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup> he also made a new, elaborate and synoptic time-scheme of the protagonists' movements.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxvi</sup>

[1945](../_/1945.md) seems to have been a hiatus period. By that time the story had several drafts and copies. The "clean" one, which was presentable and readable, was home-typed by himself or hand-written with the assistance of his children. He never parted from it, or any of its chapters (being "so closely knit"), hoping that he would find time to resume work to any of its parts.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup>

After this second long gap, he felt that he had to study his own work to be able to resume<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">[13]</a></sup> and made another push in September [1946](../_/1946.md) with Book V, which he completed by the end of [October](../O/October.md). He also made more revisions to the *Fellowship*, and it was around that time when he decided to change the [episode](../R/Riddles_in_the_Dark.md) where Bilbo wins the Ring from Gollum, in *the Hobbit*, something that was altered in the later editions.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxvii</sup>

He showed a copy of the manuscript to his publishers in 1947. The story was effectively finished the late summer of [1948](../_/1948.md), when Tolkien was vacating at Woodcote;<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxvii</sup> but he did not finish making fair copies and typescripts, revising and changing earlier parts of the work, until [October](../O/October.md) [1949](../_/1949.md). During that time he elaborated on the backstory in the Prologue and the Appendices, and made late changes, such as deciding [Arwen](../A/Arwen.md)'s name (from earlier "[Finduilas](../F/Finduilas.md)").<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxviii</sup> Almost all of the work had been typed by himself at home, as he could not afford the £100 to hire a local typist.<sup id="cite_ref-l124_14-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l124-14">[14]</a></sup>

He intended *The Silmarillion* (itself largely unrevised at this point) to be published along with *The Lord of the Rings*,<sup id="cite_ref-l124_14-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l124-14">[14]</a></sup> but his publishers, Allen and Unwin, were unwilling to do this.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">[15]</a></sup> This dispute led to the book being offered to [Collins](../H/HarperCollins.md) in [1950](../_/1950.md). His contact at Collins, [Milton Waldman](../M/Milton_Waldman.md), expressed the belief that *The Lord of the Rings* itself "urgently needed cutting", although Tolkien seems to have further expanded the [Appendix A](../A/Appendix_A.md).<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:xxx</sup> Tired of the delays, in Spring [1952](../_/1952.md) Tolkien demanded that Collins publish the book, but they declined and withdrew form the negotiations.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">[16]</a></sup> Unwin, aware of Tolkien's problems with Collins, had made known to him that his house never closed its doors.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:xxx</sup> Downhearted, worrying about the costs, and expecting a retirement in poverty, Tolkien wrote to Stanley's son, [Rayner](../R/Rayner_Unwin.md), saying "better something than nothing" and that he "would gladly consider the publication of any part of the stuff".<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">[17]</a></sup> After a warm reply from Rayner expessing his interest, Tolkien retreated once more in Woodcote to read through, further correct and update the text, resulting to some adjustments to the story's chronology.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxi</sup>

By [April](../A/April.md) [1953](../_/1953.md) Tolkien had completed the revision for press.<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">[18]</a></sup> By [1965](../_/1965.md) Tolkien continued to revise the text beyond the deadline. The text continued to be corrected as Tolkien and his son, Christopher, sent discovered errors to A&amp;U.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xl</sup>

### Illustrations and maps {#Illustrations_and_maps}

While writing the book, Tolkien created some illustrations and diagrams as concept art. But because of the prohibitive cost of colored illustrations, only the maps and simple drawings of the [Doors of Durin](../D/Doors_of_Durin.md) could be included. Three colored pages of the [Book of Mazarbul](../B/Book_of_Mazarbul.md) had to be excluded.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:xxxiii-xxxiv</sup>

Tolkien also made maps to help him with writing ("in such a story one cannot make a map for the narrative, but must first make a map and make the narrative agree") and felt that three maps were essential to help the reader and should come with the book<sup id="cite_ref-l137_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l137-19">[19]</a></sup> including a large-scale map necessary to illustrate the action of the later 2/3 parts of the book.<sup id="cite_ref-L|187_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-L|187-20">[20]</a></sup> But as of [1953](../_/1953.md), and after spending a lot of time working in vain, he felt he had not enough skill or leisure to make a readable, black and white map to fit into a page.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">[21]</a></sup>

<figure class="mw-default-size" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a class="mw-file-description" href="../../file/C/Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_(composite).gif.md"><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/thumb/9/90/Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif/300px-Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="300" height="365" class="mw-file-element" srcset="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/thumb/9/90/Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif/450px-Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif 1.5x, https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/thumb/9/90/Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif/600px-Christopher_Tolkien_-_The_First_Map_%28composite%29.gif 2x" data-file-width="1450" data-file-height="1762"/></a><figcaption>&#34;<a href="The_First_Map_of_The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md" title="The First Map of The Lord of the Rings">The First Map of The Lord of the Rings</a>&#34; by <a href="../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md" title="Christopher Tolkien">Christopher Tolkien</a>.</figcaption></figure>

Eventually [Christopher Tolkien](../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md) (who signed as C.T. or C.J.R.T.) drew the three maps, a [semi-pictorial general map](../G/General_Map_of_Middle-earth.md) of the [Westlands](../W/Westlands.md) (late [1953](../_/1953.md)), [the central parts](../A/A_Part_of_the_Shire.md) of [the Shire](The_Shire.md) (March [1954](../_/1954.md)) and [a large-scale map](../M/Map_of_Rohan,_Gondor,_and_Mordor.md) (April [1955](../_/1955.md)) focusing on the courses of Frodo, Aragorn and the Rohirrim.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:lv</sup> At first Tolkien was disappointed with the latter, because, being different from the general map, he struggled with keeping the distances and it revealed "all the chinks in the armour"; he briefly considered dropping it.<sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">[22]</a></sup> The process took several days adjusting and rescaling it, even around the clock, and then Christopher sat from "6 a.m. to 6 a.m. without bed" to re-draw it. Christopher insisted that readers would mispronounce the C in names such as [Celos](../C/Celos.md), [Ciril](../C/Ciril.md) etc, and preferred the clearer spelling with K, despite Tolkien settling with the classical spelling in the text.<sup id="cite_ref-L|187_20-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-L|187-20">[20]</a></sup>

In preparation for the publication (1953 to 1955) the drawings were for the most part redrawn by a printer's copyist, and the maps were corrected.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:xxxiii-xxxiv</sup>

Preparing the second edition, Tolkien attempted to make some corrections but ran into some difficulties with the maps; the Shire-map was the most problematic and proceeded to some corrections. For the others, he decided to adjust the narrative to agree with the maps "where this is possible and does not damage the story".<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lxi, quoting previously unpublished parts of <a href="../L/Letter_274.md" title="Letter 274">Letter 274</a></sup>

### Suggested and alternative titles {#Suggested_and_alternative_titles}

J.R.R. Tolkien contemplated numerous alternative titles for *The Lord of the Rings* either for the six parts or "Books" or its three volumes. An early title for the book was "The Magic Ring".<sup id="cite_ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23">[23]</a></sup>

Tolkien wrote to [Rayner Unwin](../R/Rayner_Unwin.md):

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>Would it not do if the &#39;book-titles&#39; were used: e.g. The Lord of the Rings: Vol. I The Ring Sets out (sic) and The Ring Goes South; Vol. II The Treason of Isengard, and The Ring goes East; Vol. III The War of the Ring, and The End of the Third Age? &#34;If not, I can at the moment think of nothing better than: I The Shadow Grows II The Ring in the Shadow III The War of the Ring or The Return of the King.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><i><a href="The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien.md" title="The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien">The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien</a></i>, <a href="../L/Letter_136.md" title="Letter 136">Letter 136</a></cite></p>
</blockquote>

A list of contents to a manuscript of *The Lord of the Rings* located at [Marquette University](../M/Marquette_University.md), has a different set of titles: Vol. I The First Journey and The Journey of the Nine Companions; Vol. II The Treason of Isengard and The Journey of the Ring-bearers; Vol. III The War of the Ring and The End of the Third Age.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">[24]</a></sup>

Tolkien [wrote again](../L/Letter_139.md) to Unwin with his new preferences: I The Return of the Shadow, II The Shadow Lengthens, and III The Return of the King. On [17 August](../_/17_August.md) he [wrote](../L/Letter_140.md) his updated choices: I The Fellowship of the Ring, II The Two Towers (deliberately ambiguous), III The War of the Ring.

Four of the proposed titles, *[The Return of the Shadow](The_Return_of_the_Shadow.md)*, *[The Treason of Isengard](The_Treason_of_Isengard.md)*, *[The War of the Ring](The_War_of_the_Ring.md)* and *[The End of the Third Age](The_End_of_the_Third_Age.md)*, were used by [Christopher Tolkien](../C/Christopher_Tolkien.md) for the volumes of *[The History of Middle-earth](The_History_of_Middle-earth.md)*.

The name of the complete work is often abbreviated to *LotR*, *LOTR*, or simply *LR*, and the three volumes as *FR*, *FOTR*, or *FotR* (The Fellowship of the Ring), *TT* or *TTT* (The Two Towers), and *RK*, *ROTK*, or *RotK* (The Return of the King).

<dl><dt>Seven-volume set</dt></dl>

A [1999](../_/1999.md) (Millennium Edition) UK 7-volume box set (<a class="internal mw-magiclink-isbn" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/wiki/Special:BookSources/0261103873">ISBN 0261103873</a>) followed the six-book division authored by Tolkien, but with the *[Appendices](../A/Appendices.md)* as a separate seventh volume. The letters T, O, L, K, I, E, N appear on the spines of the boxed set which includes a CD.

The individual names for books in this series were decided posthumously, based on suggestions Tolkien had made in [Letter 136](../L/Letter_136.md).

- *T* Book I: *The Ring Sets Out*
- *O* Book II: *The Ring Goes South*
- *L* Book III: *The Treason of Isengard*
- *K* Book IV: *The Ring Goes East*
- *I* Book V: *The War of the Ring*
- *E* Book VI: *The End of the Third Age*
- *N* Appendices

In late [1953](../_/1953.md), [Allen &amp; Unwin](../A/Allen_&_Unwin.md) asked Tolkien to create dust-jacket designs for *The Lord of the Rings*. Three were needed, because the work was divided into three volumes for reasons of cost. Tolkien proceeded to work on the book covers, creating multiple versions for each of the three volumes, before settling the design and sending them to Allen &amp; Unwin.

Although Allen &amp; Unwin chose instead to use a uniform design, with the Ring and Eye device, on each volume, in recent years (since [1997](../_/1997.md)) editions have been published with dust-jackets closely adapting Tolkien's original drawings.

For the information on the original designs and their meaning please [see here](The_Lord_of_the_Rings%252FOriginal_dust-jacket_designs.md).

## Publication {#Publication}

For publication, due largely to post-war paper shortages, but also to keep the price of the first volume down, the book was divided into three volumes (*[The Fellowship of the Ring](The_Fellowship_of_the_Ring.md)*: Books I and II; *[The Two Towers](The_Two_Towers.md)*: Books III and IV; and *[The Return of the King](The_Return_of_the_King.md)*: Books V and VI, 6 appendices). Tolkien envisioned it a single work, whose natural division was in six "Books", and thought the division in three volumes as "artificial", citing that the Books III and IV, both published in *The Two Towers*, were unrelated and divergent.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">[25]</a></sup> In retrospect he blamed partly this artificial (but unavoidable) publication as a "trilogy" for the apparent shapeless and asymmetrical tone that some critics noted.<sup id="cite_ref-l149_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l149-26">[26]</a></sup>

Delays in producing appendices and maps led to these being published later than originally hoped — on the 29 July and 11 November 1954 and 20 October 1955 in the United Kingdom, slightly later in the United States. *The Return of the King* was especially delayed. Tolkien did not, however, much like the title *The Return of the King*, believing it gave away too much of the storyline. He had originally suggested *[The War of the Ring](The_War_of_the_Ring.md)* which was dismissed by his publishers.

The books were published under a 'profit-sharing' arrangement, where Tolkien would not receive an advance or royalties until the books had broken even, but after then take a large share of the profits.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxii</sup>

In order to have a review blurb printed on the front flap of the dust-jacket, proof copies of the *Fellowship* were sent to critics including [C.S. Lewis](../C/C.S._Lewis.md), [Naomi Mitchison](../N/Naomi_Mitchison.md) and <a class="new" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/index.php?title=Richard_Hughes&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Richard Hughes (page does not exist)">Richard Hughes</a>, who wrote enthusiastic and triumphant reviews, comparing the work with classics.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lii</sup> [George Sayer](../G/George_Sayer.md) wrote a blurb for the [1953](../_/1953.md) catalogue.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxiv</sup>

The second edition was released on [27 October](../_/27_October.md) [1966](../_/1966.md), while in [1969](../_/1969.md) Allen &amp; Unwin released a deluxe, one-volume edition, as was Tolkien's original intention.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lii</sup>

An index to the entire 3-volume set at the end of third volume was promised in the first volume. However, this proved impractical to compile in a reasonable timescale. Later, in 1966, four indices which were not compiled by Tolkien were added to *The Return of the King*.

Because the three-volume binding was so widely distributed, the work is usually referred to as the *Lord of the Rings* "trilogy". Tolkien himself made use of the term "trilogy" for the work, though he did at other times consider this incorrect, as it was written and conceived as a single book.

## Influences on the book {#Influences_on_the_book}

<div class="tg-hatnote metadata noprint"><div class="tg-hatnote-icon"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/c/c6/CdxIconArticleDisambiguation.svg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="20" data-file-height="20"/></span></span></div><div class="tg-hatnote-content">See also: <a href="../J/J.R.R._Tolkien&#39;s_inspirations.md" title="J.R.R. Tolkien&#39;s inspirations">J.R.R. Tolkien&#39;s inspirations</a></div></div>

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>I wrote <i>[The Lord of the Rings]</i> as a personal satisfaction, driven to it by the scarcity of literature of the sort that I wanted to read (and what there was was often heavily alloyed).
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>J.R.R. Tolkien to <a class="mw-redirect" href="../W/W._H._Auden.md" title="W. H. Auden">W. H. Auden</a><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">[27]</a></sup></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div></div>

Tolkien had a deep desire to write a mythology for England, especially after his horrific experiences during the [Great War](../G/Great_War.md). He was also influenced by the effects of continued industrialisation, where he saw much of the England he loved passing away and became aware of the immense evil in the world. Thus to understand his writings we must be aware of how Tolkien the scholar influences Tolkien the author. His writing of this mythology emerges as an Oxford philologist well acquainted with Northern European Medieval Literature including the great mythic works such as the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hervarar_saga" title="wikipedia:Hervarar saga">Hervarar saga</a>, the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lsunga_Saga" title="wikipedia:Völsunga Saga">Völsunga saga</a>, the influential *[Beowulf](<../B/Beowulf_(poem).md>)* as well as other [Old Norse](../O/Old_Norse.md), [Old](../O/Old_English.md) and [Middle English](../M/Middle_English.md) Texts. He was also inspired by non-Germanic works such as the Finnish epic *[Kalevala](../K/Kalevala.md)*. A man who had created his first language by the age of seven, he was driven by a desire to write a mythology for England influenced by his exposure and expertise of these ancient traditions. The need for such a myth was often a topic of conversation in his meetings with the [Inklings](../I/Inklings.md), fellow Oxford scholars who have been described as Christian Romantics, meeting weekly and discussing Icelandic myths and their own unpublished compositions. Tolkien agreed with one of the other members of the group, [C.S. Lewis](../C/C.S._Lewis.md), that if there were no adequate myths for England then they would have to write their own. Tolkien's work has been commonly interpreted in this light. On the other hand, Middle-earth was not an alternate origin of the summary of English culture, but a generic European historio-myth, seen from the north-west.<sup id="cite_ref-maria_28-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-maria-28">[28]</a></sup>

*The Lord of the Rings* began as a personal exploration by Tolkien of his interests in philology, religion (particularly Roman [Catholicism](../C/Catholicism.md)); fairy tales, and [Norse](../N/Norse_mythology.md) and [Celtic](../C/Celtic.md) mythology. Tolkien detailed his creation to an astounding extent; he created a complete mythology for his realm of Middle-earth, including genealogies of characters, languages, [runes](../R/Runes.md), calendars and histories. Some of this supplementary material is detailed in the appendices to *The Lord of the Rings*, and the mythological history was woven into a large, biblically-styled volume entitled *[The Silmarillion](The_Silmarillion.md)*.

J.R.R. Tolkien once described *The Lord of the Rings* as "*a fundamentally religious and Catholic work*" he wrote to his friend, the English Jesuit Father [Robert Murray](../R/Robert_Murray.md), "*unconsciously so at first, but consciously in the revision.*"(*[The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien](The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien.md)*, 142). There are many theological themes underlying the narrative, the battle of good versus evil, the triumph of humility over pride, the activity of grace, Death and Immortality, Resurrection, Salvation, Repentance, Self-Sacrifice, Free Will, Humility, Justice, Fellowship, Authority and Healing. In it the great virtues of Mercy and Pity (shown by Bilbo and Frodo towards Gollum) win the day and the message from the Lord's Prayer "And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" was very much on Tolkien's mind as Frodo struggled against the power of the One Ring (*Letters*, 181 and 191).

Religious motifs other than Christian can be discerned as strong influences in Tolkien's Middle Earth. The pantheon of the Valar and Maiar (greater and lesser gods/angels) responsible for the creation and maintenance of everything from skies ([Manwë](../M/Manwë.md)) and seas ([Ulmo](../U/Ulmo.md)), to dreams ([Lórien](../I/Irmo.md)) and dooms ([Mandos](../M/Mandos.md)) suggest a pre-Christian mythology in style, albeit that these Valar and Maiar are themselves creations of a monotheistic entity — [Ilúvatar](../I/Ilúvatar.md) or Eru, "The One".

Other pre-Christian mythological references can be seen in the representations of: a "Green Man" — Tom Bombadil, wise-men — the Istari (commonly referred to as the Wizards, perhaps more of angels), shapechangers — Beorn, undead spirits — Barrow Wights, Oathbreakers, sentient nonhumans — Dwarves, Elves, Hobbits, and, of course, Ents. Magic is utilised freely in Middle-earth, and may be found not only in the incantations of Wizards, but in the weapons and tools of warriors and craftspeople, in the perceptions and abilities of heroes, and in the natural world itself.

Tolkien did repeatedly insist that his works were not an allegory of any kind, and even though his thoughts on the matter are mentioned in the introduction of the book, there has been heavy speculation about the Ruling Ring being an allegory for the atom bomb. However, Tolkien had already completed most of the book, and planned the ending in entirety, before the first atom bombs were made public to the world during the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. However there is a strong theme of despair in the face of new mechanized warfare that Tolkien himself had experienced in the trenches of World War One. The development of a specially bred orc army, and the destruction of the environment to aid this have modern resonances. Nevertheless, the author's own opinion on the matter of allegories was that he disliked them, and it would be irresponsible to dismiss such direct statements on these matters.

Tolkien saw critics attempting to fit his writing in some genre, and discover his sources. Actually Tolkien felt that it uses elements of various genres. Some sources attributed to it were works Tolkien never read or would not read.<sup id="cite_ref-maria_28-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-maria-28">[28]</a></sup>

Some locations and characters were inspired by Tolkien's childhood in [Sarehole](../S/Sarehole.md), then a [Warwickshire](../W/Warwickshire.md) village, now part of [Birmingham](../B/Birmingham.md), and in Birmingham itself.

## Reception {#Reception}

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>I rarely remember a book about which I have had such violent arguments. Nobody seems to have a moderate opinion; either, like myself, people find it a masterpiece of its genre or they cannot abide it
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><a href="../W/W.H._Auden.md" title="W.H. Auden">W.H. Auden</a>, <i><a href="../A/At_the_End_of_the_Quest,_Victory.md" title="At the End of the Quest, Victory">At the End of the Quest, Victory</a></i></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div></div>

Upon release of the volumes, some opinions were even enthusiastic, most positive, but there were also mixed and negative; some of them so much that Tolkien felt that those critics read his work carelessly.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. lxxiv</sup> Various reviewers likened the work to those by <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser" title="wikipedia:Edmund Spenser">Edmund Spenser</a>, <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malory" title="wikipedia:Thomas Malory">Thomas Malory</a>, <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brothers_Grimm" title="wikipedia:Brothers Grimm">Brothers Grimm</a>, <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T.H._White" title="wikipedia:T.H. White">T.H. White</a> and <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariosto" title="wikipedia:Ariosto">Ariosto</a>, the <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/pre-Raphaelites" title="wikipedia:pre-Raphaelites">pre-Raphaelites</a> as well as the <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mabinogion" title="wikipedia:Mabinogion">Mabinogion</a></i> being an amalgam of those works and styles; such grand comments put off some readers. It was thought also that it tried to balance between classical and epic words and more simplistic and childish styles, such as the <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Light_Programme" title="wikipedia:BBC Light Programme">Light Programme</a></i> and <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boy%27s_Own_Paper" title="wikipedia:Boy&#39;s Own Paper">Boy&#39;s Own Paper</a></i> (Tolkien was surprised and puzzled to see so variant and discrepant judgements on his style<sup id="cite_ref-l149_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l149-26">[26]</a></sup>). Some understood the book as an allegory of the struggle between "the <a class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" href="../W/West.md" title="West">West</a>" and "the Communist [East](../E/East.md)", with the Ring symbolizing nuclear power.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxv-xxxvii</sup>

The books were a commercial success and more copies were ordered; *The Return of the King* was printed in more copies than the first two volumes.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxv, xxxix</sup>

By the 1990s, the book came first in several opinion polls as readers' choice for best or favourite book.<sup id="cite_ref-rc_3-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-rc-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:p. xxxix</sup>

### Criticism {#Criticism}

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p><i>The Lord of the Rings</i> is<br/>
one of those things: if you<br/>
like it you do: if you don&#39;t,<br/>
then you boo!
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><a href="../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md" title="J.R.R. Tolkien">J.R.R. Tolkien</a><sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">[33]</a></sup></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div></div>

The book was characterized as "juvenile balderdash" by American critic Edmund Wilson in his essay "<a class="external text" href="http://www.jrrvf.com/sda/critiques/The_Nation.html" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Oo, those awful Orcs</a>", and in 1961 Philip Toynbee wrote, somewhat prematurely, that it had "passed into a merciful oblivion".<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">[34]</a></sup> Maurice Richardson thought that although dialogues vary among some races, every line has the same "flat voice", as there is no fleshed-out characterization or individuality.<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">[35]</a></sup> Although she had never read *The Lord of the Rings*, Germaine Greer wrote "it has been my nightmare that Tolkien would turn out to be the most influential writer of the twentieth century. The bad dream has materialized."

[W.H. Auden](../W/W.H._Auden.md) also criticized the book in a 1968 *Critical Quarterly* article, "[Good and evil in *The Lord of the Rings*](../G/Good_and_Evil_in_The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md)", objecting to Tolkien's conception of sentient species that are intrinsically evil without possibility of redemption. (This is a criticism often directed at [Dungeons and Dragons](../D/Dungeons_and_Dragons.md)-like fantasy worlds as well as at fantasy literature in general, and a criticism that Tolkien himself increasingly struggled with during his last years.) On the other hand, in a 1956 *New York Times* book review, "At the end of the Quest, Victory," Auden also called the book "a masterpiece of its genre" that "succeeded where <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton" title="wikipedia:John Milton">Milton</a> failed" in depicting an epic battle between good and evil, and wrote that it "never violated" the "reader's sense of the credible."

Edmund Fuller dismissed much of the criticism as a natural consequence, something that inevitably follows a critical acclaim.<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">[36]</a></sup>

Furthermore, like almost every fantasy book, Lord of the Rings is also accused by Evangelicals for its alleged references to quasi-pagan elements and "occultism".

Science-fiction author David Brin has criticised the books for unquestioning devotion to a traditional elitist social structure, their positive depiction of the slaughter of the opposing forces, and their romantic backward-looking worldview.<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">[37]</a></sup> These criticisms often supply also similar accusations about [racism](../R/Racism.md).

Another notable SF writer [Michael Moorcock](../M/Michael_John_Moorcock.md) wrote a long and piercing critique of the book under the title Epic Pooh advancing the thesis that it was simply a child's tale written in the language of epic myth.

China Mieville, a modern fantasy writer, criticised Tolkien's works as "reactionary." Mieville is also a detractor of later fantasy which draws heavily upon Tolkien's work, based on the idea that such work is cliché.

## Adaptations {#Adaptations}

<dl><dt>1978 film</dt></dl>

<div class="tg-hatnote metadata noprint"><div class="tg-hatnote-icon"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/9/9c/CdxIconArticle.svg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="20" data-file-height="20"/></span></span></div><div class="tg-hatnote-content"><i>Main article:</i> <b><a href="The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1978_film).md" title="The Lord of the Rings (1978 film)">The Lord of the Rings (1978 film)</a></b></div></div>

<br> This film, originally released by [United Artists](../U/United_Artists.md), was directed by [Ralph Bakshi](../R/Ralph_Bakshi.md) and used an animation technique called rotoscoping in which footage of live actors was filmed and then traced over.

The film was part one of what was originally to be a two-part adaptation of Tolkien's story, Part I ending after the battle of Helm's Deep, but before Sam, [Frodo](../F/Frodo_Baggins.md) and [Gollum](../G/Gollum.md) traverse the [Dead Marshes](../D/Dead_Marshes.md), and Part II picking up from where the first film left off. Made for a minimal budget of $8 million dollars, the film earned $30 million dollars at the box office.

United Artists viewed the film as a flop, and refused to fund a Part II (covering the rest of the story), leaving the door open for [Rankin/Bass](../R/Rankin%252FBass.md) to do the work for him.

<dl><dt>1980 film</dt></dl>

<div class="tg-hatnote metadata noprint"><div class="tg-hatnote-icon"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/9/9c/CdxIconArticle.svg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="20" data-file-height="20"/></span></span></div><div class="tg-hatnote-content"><i>Main article:</i> <b><a href="The_Return_of_the_King_(1980_film).md" title="The Return of the King (1980 film)">The Return of the King (1980 film)</a></b></div></div>

The 1980 animated television version of *[The Return of the King](<The_Return_of_the_King_(1980_film).md>)* picked up from where the book began, and not from where Bakshi's film left off. Additionally, the change in style and character design was quite noticeable.

Since this film was targeted to a younger audience, adult enthusiasts have complained that much of the depth and darkness of the book was discarded.

<dl><dt>2001-2003 films</dt></dl>

<div class="tg-hatnote metadata noprint"><div class="tg-hatnote-icon"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/9/9c/CdxIconArticle.svg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="20" data-file-height="20"/></span></span></div><div class="tg-hatnote-content"><i>Main article:</i> <b><a href="The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series).md" title="The Lord of the Rings (film series)">The Lord of the Rings (film series)</a></b></div></div>

<br> This is a series of three epic fantasy adventure films directed by [Peter Jackson](../P/Peter_Jackson.md). The films are subtitled *The Fellowship of the Ring* ([2001](../_/2001.md)), *The Two Towers* ([2002](../_/2002.md)), and *The Return of the King* ([2003](../_/2003.md)). Produced and distributed by [New Line Cinema](../N/New_Line_Cinema.md) with the co-production of WingNut Films, the series is an international venture between New Zealand and the United States. The films feature an ensemble cast including Elijah Wood, Ian McKellen, Liv Tyler, Viggo Mortensen, Sean Astin, Cate Blanchett, John Rhys-Davies, Christopher Lee, Billy Boyd, Dominic Monaghan, Orlando Bloom, Hugo Weaving, Andy Serkis and Sean Bean.

<dl><dt>Radio</dt></dl>

The [BBC](../B/BBC.md) produced a [13-part radio adaptation](<The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1956_radio_series).md>) of *The Lord of the Rings* in 1955. It was panned by Tolkien, complaining about the "sillification" of it and the changing of characters, such as Old Man Willow in league with Mordor and Goldberry being Tom Bombadil's daughter rather than his wife. No recording has survived to date.<sup class="noprint" style="line-height: 0.4em;">[<a href="../../help/R/References.md" title="Help:References"><i>source?</i></a>]</sup>

A [1979 dramatization](<The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1979_radio_series).md>) was broadcast in the USA and subsequently issued on tape and CD. In [1981](../_/1981.md) the BBC broadcast a [new, ambitious dramatization](<The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1981_radio_series).md>) of *The Lord of the Rings* in 26 half-hour instalments.

<dl><dt>Stage</dt></dl>

<div class="tg-hatnote metadata noprint"><div class="tg-hatnote-icon"><span typeof="mw:File"><span><img src="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/images/9/9c/CdxIconArticle.svg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="16" height="16" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="20" data-file-height="20"/></span></span></div><div class="tg-hatnote-content"><i>Main article:</i> <b><a href="The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(musical).md" title="The Lord of the Rings (musical)">The Lord of the Rings (musical)</a></b></div></div>

Mirvish Productions made a stage musical adaptation of *The Lord of the Rings*, written by Shaun McKenna and Matthew Warchus with music by A. R. Rahman and Värttinä, collaborating with Christopher Nightingale, being directed by Matthew Warchus. It opened on [23 March](../_/23_March.md) [2006](../_/2006.md) at Toronto's Princess of Wales Theatre, and in London in autumn 2006.
