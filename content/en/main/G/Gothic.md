**Gothic** was the language of the Goths; the earliest recorded Germanic language,<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Note6_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Note6-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chapter_1_Lit_p._18_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chapter_1_Lit_p._18-3">[3]</a></sup> it is now extinct and left no descendants. As a Germanic language, Gothic was related to [Old English](/wiki/Old_English) and [Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse).

Gothic was the first language that [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) studied for his own pleasure. He also disovered modern historical philology in Gothic.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> Around [1910](/wiki/1910) or later, Tolkien had bought his tutor [Joseph Wright](/wiki/Joseph_Wright)'s <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_of_the_Gothic_Language" title="wikipedia:Grammar of the Gothic Language">Primer of the Gothic Language</a></i> from a school-friend (who had mistaken it for a Bible Society product). He had been fascinated by Gothic and started "converting" words of other Germanic languages into Gothic script, which he wrote in his book, and signed as *Ruginwaldus Dwalakōneis* (gothicized "Ronald Tolkien"). Tolkien was also aware of the tragic history of the Goths.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>

Gothic may have given an impulsion in the first development of [Qenya](/wiki/Qenya). Tolkien even attempted to reconstruct some parts of the language and such elements survived in [Taliska](/wiki/Taliska), the language he created for the [Edain](/wiki/Edain) of the [First Age](/wiki/First_Age). He also composed a full poem, *[Bagmē Blōma](/wiki/Bagm%C4%93_Bl%C5%8Dma)* "The Flower of the Trees" in Gothic.<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> [Tom Shippey](/wiki/Tom_Shippey) suggests that Edward Gibbon's *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* stayed in Tolkien's mind and that The Gothic History of Jordanes translated by C.C. Mierow was probably one of the most interesting Latin histories that Edward Gibbon had used.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordanes" title="wikipedia:Jordanes">Jordanes</a> was a latin-speaking German of Gotic descent who wrote <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getica" title="wikipedia:Getica">a history of the Goths</a> in latin.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup> Tolkien also bought all volumes of Hermann Schneider's *Germanische Heldensage* and wrote that Gothic was studied "as a main source of the poetic inspiration of ancient England and the North".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup>

According to [Lisa Star](/wiki/Lisa_Star), Tolkien also devised a [tengwar](/wiki/Tengwar "Tengwar") mode for Gothic which is known to exist, but remain unpublished to date.<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">[10]</a></sup>

<h2><span id="Gothic_names_in_Tolkien.27s_legendarium"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Gothic_names_in_Tolkien&#39;s_legendarium">Gothic names in Tolkien&#39;s legendarium</span></h2>

Since in *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)* Old English represents [Rohanese](/wiki/Rohanese), Tolkien used Gothic to represent the earlier language of the [Northmen](/wiki/Northmen) of [Rhovanion](/wiki/Rhovanion) in accordance to the similarity between these languages.<sup id="cite_ref-Note6_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Note6-2">[2]</a></sup> However, in reality Old English was not directly related to Gothic as the way Rohirric was to the language of the Northmen; therefore Tolkien's simulation does not reflect exactly the relationship between these languages but rather their similarity. As a consequence, the names of the early kings and princes of the Northmen are of Gothic origin.<sup id="cite_ref-Note6_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Note6-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-Chapter_1_Lit_p._18_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Chapter_1_Lit_p._18-3">[3]</a></sup>

- [Vidugavia](/wiki/Vidugavia)
- [Vidumavi](/wiki/Vidumavi)
- [Vinitharya](/wiki/Vinitharya)
- [Marhwini](/wiki/Marhwini)
- [Marhari](/wiki/Marhari)
- [Forthwini](/wiki/Forthwini)

Tolkien also used names of Gothic and Frankish origin that are still in use or appear in real-world history for first-names of old [Hobbit](/wiki/Hobbit) families, especially those of [Fallowhide](/wiki/Fallohides "Fallohides") origin, such as the [Tooks](/wiki/Tooks) and the [Bolgers](/wiki/Bolgers).<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup> One example from the Bolger family tree is <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odoacer" title="wikipedia:Odoacer">Odovacar</a>, the name of a 5th century ruler of Italy of Germanic descent. In addition, Tolkien also used the names Roderic, Alaric, Theodoric and Athanaric in the earliest version of the Brandybuck family tree,<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup> which are of Gothic origin.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">[13]</a></sup> <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roderic" title="wikipedia:Roderic">Roderic</a>, <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaric_I" title="wikipedia:Alaric I">Alaric</a>, <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodoric_the_Great" title="wikipedia:Theodoric the Great">Theodoric</a> and <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanaric" title="wikipedia:Athanaric">Athanaric</a> are the names of kings of the Goths.

It is noteworthy that many Gothic names, which were used by Tolkien can be found with the same or similar spelling in Mierow's translation of Jordanes' History of the Goths. Among those are <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinithar" title="wikipedia:Vinithar">Vinitharius</a>, Alaric, Theodoric, Athanaric and Odoacer.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">[14]</a></sup>
