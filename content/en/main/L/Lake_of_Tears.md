The **Lake of Tears**<sup id="cite_ref-Lake_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lake-1">[1]</a></sup> was a wide lake of an unspecified substance that lay at the bottom of "a deep dale" that [Smith Smithson](/wiki/Smith_Smithson) saw only once in the land of [Faery](/wiki/Faery).<sup id="cite_ref-Smith_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Smith-2">[2]</a></sup>

## History {#History}

Whilst journeying to find the [King's Tree](/wiki/King%27s_Tree) again, Smith climbed "into the <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Outer_Faery&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Outer Faery (page does not exist)">Outer Mountains</a>" and stumbled upon a lake. The lake emitted a light that "was like a red sunset" and the woods surrounding it were stirred by a breeze. When Smith looked into the lake from a low overhanging cliff, he discovered that it was immeasurably deep and that "fiery creatures" swam in it among "strange shapes of flame bending and branching and wavering like great weeds". When Smith tried to enter the lake, he "fell heavily" and found that, upon stepping on it, "it was not water" but was instead something that "was harder than stone and sleeker than glass". As a consequence, "a ringing boom…echoed" and caused the surrounding breeze to become a <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Wild_Wind&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Wild Wind (page does not exist)">wild Wind</a> that drove him out of that dale. When Smith tried to save himself by clinging to "a young [birch](/wiki/Birch "Birch")", the Wind tore off every leaf before moving on, causing the birch to weep bitter tears "from its branches like rain". When Smith tried to apologize, the birch demanded that he leave and never return to Faery. While Smith obeyed the birch for a time, he eventually returned to Faery.<sup id="cite_ref-Smith_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Smith-2">[2]</a></sup>

## Other versions {#Other_versions}

In the earliest drafts of *[Smith of Wootton Major](/wiki/Smith_of_Wootton_Major)*, the Lake of Tears was "clearly liquid" that was fluid enough for Smith to "swim in it".<sup id="cite_ref-Afterword_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Afterword-3">[3]</a></sup> In the second draft, The lake became "harder than stone and slidder than glass" before "slidder" became "sleeker" in the third draft.<sup id="cite_ref-Afterword_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Afterword-3">[3]</a></sup> In the earliest draft, there was the great island in the middle and the water tasted "cool and sweet".<sup id="cite_ref-Lake_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lake-1">[1]</a></sup> In the second draft, the taste of the water was described as bitter and the tears of multiple trees (as opposed to a single birch) are described as gathering "in rivulets that ran down into the lake".<sup id="cite_ref-Lake_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lake-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Background {#Background}

In her Afterword, [Verlyn Flieger](/wiki/Verlyn_Flieger) listed the Lake of Tears drafts as an example of "how Tolkien expanded *Smith of Wootton Major* from the inside".<sup id="cite_ref-Afterword_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Afterword-3">[3]</a></sup> In a note to the words "Slidder than glass", Flieger noted that *slidder* is "an archaic adjective now obsolete" meaning "slippery; on which one readily slips".<sup id="cite_ref-note_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-note-4">[4]</a></sup> The word "comes from [Old English](/wiki/Old_English) *slidor* from *slidan*" ("slide").<sup id="cite_ref-note_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-note-4">[4]</a></sup> She further explains that the word was first used in "*Is by oferceald unzemetum slidor*" ("Ice is excessively cold, immeasurably slippery") from "the Anglo-Saxon *Runic Poem* #29" early in the eighth or ninth centuries, though the earliest usage to how Tolkien used it "occurs in <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_de_Brunne" title="wikipedia:Robert de Brunne">Robert Mannynge</a> of <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunne" title="wikipedia:Brunne">Brunne&#39;s</a> [Middle English](/wiki/Middle_English) text <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handlyng_Synne" title="wikipedia:Handlyng Synne">Handling Synne</a></i> ("Sins' Handbook")" around 1303 in which a *brygge* ("bridge") is described "as sledyr as any glas".<sup id="cite_ref-note_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-note-4">[4]</a></sup>
