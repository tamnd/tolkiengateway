On [21 October](/wiki/21_October) [1963](/wiki/1963), [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) wrote **[a letter](/wiki/Letters_not_published_in_The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien "Letters not published in The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien") to Mrs. Munby**.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

- **Subject:** Writing to a Mrs Munby in response to a number of questions posed by her son Stephen about *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)*, Tolkien discusses various topics.
- **Description:** 6 pages, 8vo, Hotel Miramar, Bournemouth and [76 Sandfield Road](/wiki/76_Sandfield_Road), Oxford, 21 October 1963, written in black ink with later clarification of handwriting by Tolkien in red ink.<sup id="cite_ref-Sotheby_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sotheby-2">[2]</a></sup>
- **Publication:** Excerpts and a description of the letter appeared in *[Sotheby's Literature and Illustration 11-12 July 2002](/wiki/Sotheby%27s_Literature_and_Illustration_11-12_July_2002)* and in <i><a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Sotheby%27s_Literature,_History_and_Children%27s_Books_12_December_2002&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Sotheby&#39;s Literature, History and Children&#39;s Books 12 December 2002 (page does not exist)">Sotheby&#39;s Literature, History and Children&#39;s Books 12 December 2002</a></i>. An excerpt from the letter was published by [Josh B. Long](/wiki/Josh_B._Long) in his article in [*Mythlore* 121/122](/wiki/Mythlore_121/122 "Mythlore 121/122") (page 35).

Comments by [Henry Gee](/wiki/Henry_Gee):<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>The letter is long, but in one place reads as follows: &#39;There must have been orc-women. But in stories that seldom if ever see the Orcs except as soldiers of armies in the service of the evil lords we naturally would not learn much about their lives. Not much was known&#39;. Tolkien also goes on to discuss the use of the word &#39;goblin&#39;: &#39;In The Hobbit &#39;goblin&#39; is used... but goblin is a fairly modern word, and very vague in its application to any sort of bogey in the dark.&#39;
</p>
</blockquote>

Comments from [Sotheby's](/wiki/Sotheby%27s):<sup id="cite_ref-Sotheby_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Sotheby-2">[2]</a></sup>

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>Blaming &#34;the combined pressure of overwork and ill health&#34; for his late response, Tolkien notes that &#34;satisfactory answers&#34; to Stephen Munby&#39;s questions &#34;would take pages, and would I think not be really intelligible to him&#34;. Tolkien therefore suggests:
<br/>
&#34;...the best general answer is that &#34;The Lord of the Rings&#34; is purely imaginary in scene and time (though not in the behaviour of the characters in the situations that they find themselves in). It is not possible to represent an &#34;imaginary&#34; world, and have answers for all the questions that may be asked about it...&#34;
<br/>
<i>Noting that Stephen Munby&#39;s questions are mainly concerned with &#34;ancient history&#34;, Tolkien states his &#34;points will all be made clearer when (if ever) I have time to complete and publish the legends of the earlier ages&#34;. The author then states his ideas of imaginary history and the layers of history behind this and, thereby, provides AN EXAMPLE OF HIS TREATMENT OF MYTHS AND HISTORY:</i> 
<br/>
&#34;...The references (such as <a href="/wiki/Morgoth" title="Morgoth">Morgoth</a> etc.) do refer to stories actually written, but in this tale they only appear in order to give the tale a &#39;place&#39; in an imaginary history. Perhaps this could be understood by a comparison with real &#39;history&#39;. Supposing a story was written in which the climax was the tragedy of the Battle of Hastings. It would be about the behaviour of the chief characters such as Harold and William, but there might be references to older history (at more than one remove) say to the death of Arthur, or the coming of the English when they were the invaders, and even far away to the legendary North from which they came, and to their struggle with the Danes (from whom William was descended) or to their old pagan gods. But it would be impossible to provide answers for such references except by writing more books!&#34;
<br/>
<i>Tolkien then confesses he does not enjoy &#34;stories of an imaginary world that have not got any imaginary history&#34;. Returning to </i>The Lord of the Rings<i>, Tolkien notes &#34;...the story is really about Death and pity and Self-sacrifice&#34; and comments on the apparent absence of God in the book.</i>
<br/>
<i>There then follow eight numbered replies to original questions that concern actual history, use of <a href="/wiki/Old_English" title="Old English">Old English</a> for the <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Rohan_language" title="Rohan language">language of the Rohirrim</a>, Satan (&#34;<a href="/wiki/Sauron" title="Sauron">Sauron</a> was one of the lesser spirits who had at one time served him...&#34;), the &#34;ultimate history of <a href="/wiki/Men" title="Men">Men</a>&#34;, orc-women, the invention of the name &#34;<a href="/wiki/Orcs" title="Orcs">orc</a>&#34; (&#34;...in <a href="/wiki/The_Hobbit" title="The Hobbit">The Hobbit</a> &#39;<a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Orc#Etymology" title="Orc">goblin</a>&#39; is used... but goblin is a fairly modern word, and very vague in its application to any sort of bogey in the dark...&#34;) and <a href="/wiki/Calaquendi" title="Calaquendi">high-elves</a>, etc.</i>
</p>
</blockquote>
