<i><b>Ferrum et Sanguis: 1914</b></i>, or simply <i><b>Ferrum et Sanguis</b></i>,<sup id="cite_ref-index_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-index-1">[1]</a></sup> is a poem with a "theme of darkness"<sup id="cite_ref-chronology1914_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology1914-2">[2]</a></sup> written by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) on [22 December](/wiki/22_December) of [1914](/wiki/1914).<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-3">[3]</a></sup>

## Poem excerpt {#Poem_excerpt}

<div style="font-style:italic; margin-left:20px;" class="poem">
<p><span class="mw-poem-indented" style="display: inline-block; margin-inline-start: 1em;">The earth and all that dwell thereon are dark in a great gloom:</span><br/>
<span class="mw-poem-indented" style="display: inline-block; margin-inline-start: 1em;">A frightful hand has dared its shade across the blackened sun,</span><br/>
<span class="mw-poem-indented" style="display: inline-block; margin-inline-start: 1em;">And, lest the stars come out and still do light us in its room,</span><br/>
<span class="mw-poem-indented" style="display: inline-block; margin-inline-start: 1em;">Has fouled the heavens with his touch, or plucked them one by one<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup></span>
</p>
</div>

## Background {#Background}

On [22 December](/wiki/22_December) of [1914](/wiki/1914), probably<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> a day after writing the poem *[Dark](/wiki/Dark)*,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup> [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) wrote the poem, **1914**,<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> the earliest<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> draft of *Ferrum et Sanguis: 1914*.<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-3">[3]</a></sup> Tolkien inscribed the poem's first manuscript with the [Latin](/wiki/Latin) phrases "Ex umbris et loco tenebroso" ("out of the shadows and the dark place") and "De Profundis" ("out of the depths").<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> Later, Tolkien made a "slightly revised version" in which he separated many of the lines in two and renamed the poem to *Ferrum et Sanguis*<sup id="cite_ref-index_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-index-1">[1]</a></sup> ("iron and blood")<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-3">[3]</a></sup> in [Latin](/wiki/Latin).<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> Even later, Tolkien rewrote the poem in pencil on two new manuscripts, naming it *Ferrum et Sanguis: 1914*<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-3">[3]</a></sup> on both and making minor revisions.<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup>

In [2014](/wiki/2014), Raymond Edwards suggested in his [biography of Tolkien](</wiki/Tolkien_(2014_book)> "Tolkien (2014 book)") that Tolkien's poem is related "to the political situation of [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I)".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> In [September](/wiki/September) of [2024](/wiki/2024), the poem was published for the first time as entry 21 in *[The Collected Poems of J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/The_Collected_Poems_of_J.R.R._Tolkien)*.<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> In their commentary, [Wayne G. Hammond](/wiki/Wayne_G._Hammond) and [Christina Scull](/wiki/Christina_Scull) explain that Tolkien's poem, like *[Dark](/wiki/Dark)*,<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> had a "theme of darkness"<sup id="cite_ref-chronology1914_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology1914-2">[2]</a></sup> concerning the [Great War](/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") but, unlike the earlier poem,<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> *Ferrum et Sanguis: 1914*<sup id="cite_ref-chronology_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-chronology-3">[3]</a></sup> reflected the "sinking of spirits which he projected onto the whole of the Earth and even the heavens".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> They also explain that the phrase "iron and blood" is "from a speech by the nineteenth-century German leader <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Bismarck" title="wikipedia:Otto von Bismarck">Otto von Bismarck</a>" referring "to the use of military power to unify Germany and extend its territory" and that the Latin phrase "De Profundis…are the first words of one of the penitential psalms ('Out of the depths I call to you, Lord; Lord, hear my cry!'), a prayer for the dead".<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> Hammond and Scull further suggest that, in using the Latin phrase "ex umbris et loco tenebroso", Tolkien might have been thinking of the "Tenebrae" ("service of shadows"), a service "conducted by the Catholic Church in Holy Week, in which candles are gradually extinguished until the room is in total darkness".<sup id="cite_ref-collectedpoems_4-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-collectedpoems-4">[4]</a></sup> In an article in an issue from the *Journal of Tolkien Research*, Patrick J. Lyon suggested that the poem references the "O Antiphon" for [21 December](/wiki/21_December) from "the Catholic Advent Vespers" instead of, "or in addition to"<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup>, the "Tenebrae".<sup id="cite_ref-Lyon_9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Lyon-9">[9]</a></sup>
