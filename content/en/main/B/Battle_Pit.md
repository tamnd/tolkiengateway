The **Battle Pit** was "an old sand-pit" near the town of [Bywater](Bywater.md) in [the Shire](../T/The_Shire.md) that was used as the burial place for the [Ruffians](../R/Ruffians.md) killed during the [Battle of Bywater](Battle_of_Bywater.md).<sup id="cite_ref-scouring_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scouring-1">[1]</a></sup>

In *[The Atlas of Middle-earth](../T/The_Atlas_of_Middle-earth.md)*, [Karen Wynn Fonstad](../K/Karen_Wynn_Fonstad.md) speculated that the Battle Pit was located a short distance east of the town.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>

## History {#History}

On [3 November](../_/3_November.md) [T.A.](../T/Third_Age.md) [3019](../T/Third_Age_3019.md), 100 [Ruffians](../R/Ruffians.md) fought against 300 [Hobbits](../H/Hobbits.md) on the [Bywater Road](Bywater_Road.md). The Ruffians had foolishly walked into a trap within a banked portion of the road, which the Hobbits secured with barricades at either end. In the ensuing fight nearly seventy of the [Men](../M/Men.md) died. The victorious Hobbits loaded the bodies of their foes into wagons and hauled them to an old, nearby sand-pit for burial, which thereafter was called the Battle Pit. Nineteen Hobbits also died in the fray but they were buried separately, with honor in a hill-side grave above the Battle Pit. Sometime later, "a great stone was" placed over the hill-side grave and a garden grew around it.<sup id="cite_ref-scouring_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scouring-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Other versions of the legendarium {#Other_versions_of_the_legendarium}

In an early draft of the chapter "[The Scouring of the Shire](../T/The_Scouring_of_the_Shire.md)", the burial place of the Ruffians was originally "an old gravel-pit" that the Hobbits later named the **Battle Pits**. In this draft, there were no Hobbit casualties.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>

## Inspiration {#Inspiration}

In relation to the "great stone"<sup id="cite_ref-scouring_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-scouring-1">[1]</a></sup> it was noted by Patricia Reynolds that while "the setting of memorial stones . . . is a widespread tradition", the garden that grew around the stone recalls a [World War I](../W/World_War_I.md) tradition where "many English villages" would plant a "garden of remembrance" around their war-memorials.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>
