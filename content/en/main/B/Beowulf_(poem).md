<i><b>Beowulf</b></i> is the conventional title of an [Anglo-Saxon](/wiki/Old_English "Old English") epic poem.

## Tolkien and *Beowulf* {#Tolkien_and_Beowulf}

<figure class="mw-halign-left" typeof="mw:File/Thumb"><a class="mw-file-description" href="/wiki/File:John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg"><img src="/w/images/thumb/9/99/John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg/200px-John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg" decoding="async" loading="lazy" width="200" height="139" class="mw-file-element" srcset="/w/images/thumb/9/99/John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg/300px-John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg 1.5x, /w/images/thumb/9/99/John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg/400px-John_Howe_-_Beowulf_Battles_Grendel%27s_Mother.jpg 2x" data-file-width="534" data-file-height="371"/></a><figcaption><a href="/wiki/John_Howe" title="John Howe">John Howe</a> - <i>Beowulf Battles Grendel&#39;s Mother</i></figcaption></figure>

[J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) was a prominent *Beowulf* scholar. His *[Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics](/wiki/Beowulf:_The_Monsters_and_the_Critics)* was a turning point in the modern study of the poem, moving the focus from its historical to its literary significance.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup> Tolkien also left two substantial unpublished manuscripts concerning *Beowulf*. The first, a more substantial version of the previously mentioned critical essay, was edited by [Michael D.C. Drout](/wiki/Michael_D.C._Drout) and published as *[Beowulf and the Critics](/wiki/Beowulf_and_the_Critics)*.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup> The second is a partial poetic and full prose translation of the epic, including commentary.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup> The latter was a minor media sensation on its re-discovery in 2003 and was also to be prepared for publication by Drout, but as of 2010 this was not forthcoming.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup>

On 22 May 2014, HarperCollins published [Beowulf: A Translation and Commentary](/wiki/Beowulf:_A_Translation_and_Commentary), taken from the unpublished manuscript kept at the [Bodleian Library](/wiki/Bodleian_Library) in [Oxford](/wiki/Oxford).<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>

An excerpt from *Beowulf*, rendered by Tolkien in [Valmaric script](/wiki/Valmaric_script), was published with commentary in [*Parma Eldalamberon* 14](/wiki/Parma_Eldalamberon_14 "Parma Eldalamberon 14").<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> A similar excerpt from *Beowulf*, rendered by Tolkien in his <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Qenya_Alphabet&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Qenya Alphabet (page does not exist)">Qenya Alphabet</a>, was published with commentary in [*Parma Eldalamberon* 20](/wiki/Parma_Eldalamberon_20 "Parma Eldalamberon 20").<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup>

## Influence on the legendarium {#Influence_on_the_legendarium}

Tolkien looked highly upon *Beowulf*, and it both directly and indirectly influenced his own [imaginative work](/wiki/Legendarium "Legendarium"). The episode in *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)* where Bilbo steals a cup from [Smaug](/wiki/Smaug)'s hoard, for example, is a conscious homage to a similar theft in *Beowulf*.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup>
