**Eucatastrophe** is a neologism coined by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) from Greek *ευ-* ("good") and *καταστροφή* ("sudden turn").

In essence, a eucatastrophe is a massive turn in fortune from a seemingly unconquerable situation to an unforeseen victory, usually brought by grace rather than heroic effort. Such a turn is catastrophic in the sense of its breadth and surprise and positive in that a great evil or misfortune is averted.

## Coining of the term {#Coining_of_the_term}

In his [1947](/wiki/1947) essay *[On Fairy-Stories](/wiki/On_Fairy-Stories),* Tolkien described the concept:

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r395753">.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider{margin-top:1rem;margin-bottom:0.5rem;color:var(--tg-color-subtle);display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:space-between}.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider:not(:empty){gap:1rem;white-space:nowrap}.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider::before,.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider::after{content:"";height:1px;width:100%;background-color:var(--tg-border-color-base)}</style><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>I would venture to assert that all complete fairy-stories must have [a Happy Ending]. At least I would say that Tragedy is the true form of Drama, its highest function; but the opposite is true of Fairy-story. Since we do not appear to possess a word that expresses this opposite — I will call it <i>Eucatastrophe</i>. The <i>eucatastrophic</i> tale is the true form of fairy-tale, and its highest function.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>Tolkien in <a href="/wiki/On_Fairy-Stories" title="On Fairy-Stories">On Fairy-Stories</a></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/></div>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

Tolkien would go on to say that the joyous "turn" of a Happy Ending is a "sudden and miraculous grace" that is never relied on to happen again. The use of this "eucatastrophe" does not prevent its opposite - the "dyscotastrophe" of sorrow and failure - from occurring. The possibility of dyscotastrophe is necessary to the joy that occurs when eucatastrophe is employed in a tale. In his view, a good fairy-story, however dire its circumstances, can make a person catch their breath when the eucatastrophic miraculous "turn" of a Happy Ending occurs. <sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

Tolkien would further elaborate on eucatastrophes in [one of his letters](/wiki/Letter_89 "Letter 89"):

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>(...)I was there led to the view that [eucatastrophe] produces [joy at the turn of a Happy Ending] because it is a sudden glimpse of Truth, your whole nature chained in material cause and effect, the chain of death, feels a sudden relief as if a major limb out of joint had suddenly snapped back. (...) the Resurrection was the greatest &#39;eucatastrophe&#39; possible in the greatest Fairy Story – and produces that essential emotion: Christian joy which produces tears because it is qualitatively so like sorrow, because it comes from those places where Joy and Sorrow are at one, reconciled, as selfishness and altruism are lost in Love.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>Tolkien in <a href="/wiki/Letter_89" title="Letter 89">Letter 89</a></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/></div>

## Within the legendarium {#Within_the_legendarium}

Tolkien himself made use of the trope of eucatastrophe several times in his own writings. The most famous usage is the final destruction of the [One Ring](/wiki/One_Ring) within [Mount Doom](/wiki/Mount_Doom). Frodo was unable to destroy the Ring by his own strength. The Ring is destroyed by the intervention of [Ilúvatar](/wiki/Il%C3%BAvatar) after it is taken from Frodo by [Gollum](/wiki/Gollum), who Frodo himself spared earlier in the story. Had Gollum not been there, the Ring may only have been destroyed by Frodo casting himself into the fire, if it was destroyed at all.

The other famous example of a eucatastrophe is the War of Wrath fought on behalf of the Eldar at the end of the [First Age](/wiki/First_Age). [Eärendil](/wiki/E%C3%A4rendil) was able to reach [Valinor](/wiki/Valinor) and persuade the [Valar](/wiki/Valar) to go to war against [Morgoth](/wiki/Morgoth) for the sake of [Elves](/wiki/Elves) and [Men](/wiki/Men). Without the intervention of the Valar, Morgoth would never have been defeated by any mortal strength.

## Luck {#Luck}

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>You don&#39;t really suppose, do you, that all your adventures and escapes were managed by mere luck, just for your sole benefit?&#34;
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><a href="/wiki/Gandalf" title="Gandalf">Gandalf</a> to <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Bilbo" title="Bilbo">Bilbo</a><sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/></div>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

**Luck** is a concept recognizing success or failure apparently brought about by chance rather than through direct action. [Bilbo Baggins](/wiki/Bilbo_Baggins) was born with a fair share of it, far exceeding the usual allowance, as [Thorin](/wiki/Thorin) remarked.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> [Elrond](/wiki/Elrond) noted that he did not think it was by chance that many strangers came at the same time to his [council](/wiki/Council_of_Elrond "Council of Elrond"), but "*rather that it is so ordered that we, who sit here, and none others, must now find counsel for the peril of the world.*"<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>

## Inspiration {#Inspiration}

Tolkien scholar Joseph Pierce, referencing Tolkien's [Catholicism](/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), argues that "luck" is a euphemism for "a supernatural dimension to the unfolding of events in [Middle-earth](/wiki/Middle-earth), in which Tolkien shows the mystical balance that exists between the promptings of grace or of demonic temptation and the response of the will to such promptings and temptations. This mystical relationship plays itself out in the form of transcendent Providence, which is much more than 'luck' or chance."<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup>
