<i><b>Errantry</b></i> is a [Hobbit](../H/Hobbit.md) poem which was evidently composed by [Bilbo Baggins](../B/Bilbo_Baggins.md), shortly after his return from the [Lonely Mountain](../L/Lonely_Mountain.md) in [T.A.](../T/Third_Age.md) [2941](../T/Third_Age_2941.md),<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup> and probably having heard Elvish tales of the [First Age](../F/First_Age.md), but without treating them seriously. The attribution to Bilbo is made because of its similarity to the *[Eärendillinwë](Eärendillinwë.md)*, which is believed to be a version of this poem, transformed and applied to the legend of [Eärendil](Eärendil.md).<sup id="cite_ref-Preface_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Preface-2">[2]</a></sup>

While it referred to original [Elvish](Elvish.md) names, they were probably fictitious.

## Structure {#Structure}

*Errantry* was actually one of the cyclical nonsense poems which amused [Hobbits](../H/Hobbits.md), although this one is the longest and most elaborate of the kind found in the [Red Book](../R/Red_Book.md).<sup id="cite_ref-Preface_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Preface-2">[2]</a></sup>

The poem has complex trisyllabic (near-)assonances<sup id="cite_ref-l133_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l133-3">[3]</a></sup> with an original metre invented by Bilbo, and was obviously proud of them. Such do not appear in other pieces in the Red Book.<sup id="cite_ref-Preface_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Preface-2">[2]</a></sup>

Each stanza is supposed to be read first at speed and then slow down to pronounce words with clarity, with the exception of the last stanza that must begin slowly.<sup id="cite_ref-ti_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ti-4">[4]</a></sup>

## Summary {#Summary}

In the beginning of the poem, the protagonist prepares to go on an adventure, building a boat filled with “yellow oranges and porridge”. The protagonist heads off, calling upon the winds of argosies to help him pass seventeen rivers in his way. After crossing the final river, Derrilyn, he abandoned the boat to cross on foot through meadows to the nearby Shadow-land, before moving along again.

Eventually, the protagonist took a rest, deciding to sing. There he fell in love with a [butterfly](../B/Butterfly.md) who ignored him; he managed to follow and trap her, made for her pavilions to live in and offered her gifts but she continued to refuse them, until he sadly had to leave her and move on.

Passing through elven-like lands, he makes some armor and weapons and is challenged by Elven-knights. He moves on and fights "the dragon-flies of <a class="new" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/index.php?title=Paradise&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Paradise (page does not exist)">Paradise</a>" including [Hummerhorns](../H/Hummerhorns.md), [Dumbledors](../D/Dumbledors.md), and [Honeybees](../H/Honeybees.md), eventually winning the <a class="new" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/index.php?title=Golden_Honeycomb&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Golden Honeycomb (page does not exist)">Golden Honeycomb</a>. He sails home in a ship made "of leaves and gossamer" that has a "blossom for a canopy", before tarrying a bit in uninhabited little isles. Back there, he realizes that the wandering and adventuring made him forget his message and errand, and he prepares to depart again.<sup id="cite_ref-Errantry_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Errantry-5">[5]</a></sup>

## Inspiration {#Inspiration}

Tolkien felt the need to compose the poem in an attempt to use the model of the nursery rhyme *What is the rhyme to porringer?*<sup id="cite_ref-ti_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ti-4">[4]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-ABcomm_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ABcomm-10">[10]</a></sup> The meter is his own invention (using trisyllabic assonances or near-assonances) and never wrote another in this style.<sup id="cite_ref-l133_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l133-3">[3]</a></sup> This fact passed into the legendarium, as the Preface to the *Adventures of Tom Bombadil* says that Bilbo was probably proud of his meter and used it as a model for the *Eärendillinwë*.

It is a three-page long poem first published on [9 November](../_/9_November.md) [1933](../_/1933.md) in *The Oxford Magazine*. Tolkien himself considered it his most attractive poem.

## Reception {#Reception}

[Warren Lewis](../W/Warren_Lewis.md) (brother of [C.S. Lewis](../C/C.S._Lewis.md)) found it "excellent in itself" and considered Tolkien's metric invention very interesting and "a real discovery"<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup>

By [1950](../_/1950.md) the poem became famous outside Tolkien's environment and circulated anonymously in print and "folklore": a lady unknown to Tolkien heard it somewhere and was so taken by the words that traced its origin to the English Universities and ultimately to Tolkien, surprising him. Comparing the version the lady knew against the original, Tolkien noticed that the "hard words" are preserved more in the "oral tradition".<sup id="cite_ref-l133_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-l133-3">[3]</a></sup>

This poem was set to music by [Donald Swann](../D/Donald_Swann.md). The sheet music and an audio recording are part of the song-cycle *[The Road Goes Ever On](<../T/The_Road_Goes_Ever_On_(book).md>)*.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup>
