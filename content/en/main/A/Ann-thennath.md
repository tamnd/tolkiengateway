The **Ann-thennath** was one of the three Elvish [Verse Modes](../V/Verse_Modes.md). The other two being the *[Minlamad thent/estent](../M/Minlamad_thent%252Festent.md)*, and the *[Linnod](../L/Linnod.md)*.

## History {#History}

The verse form was used in the [Song of Beren and Lúthien](../S/Song_of_Beren_and_Lúthien.md) as chanted by [Strider](../S/Strider.md) to the [Hobbits](../H/Hobbits.md) beneath [Weathertop](../W/Weathertop.md). He stated that it was *”hard to render in our [Common Speech](../C/Common_Speech.md)”*.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The *ann-thennath* can be broken up as follows: [Sindarin](../S/Sindarin.md) *[ann](And.md)* = long, <i><a class="new" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/index.php?title=Thenn&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Thenn (page does not exist)">thenn</a></i> = short, *[-ath](../_/-ath.md)* = collective plural. *Thennath* could be related to the Elvish root *ten*. In this case, the *ann-thennath* could mean *long lines*.

## Inspiration {#Inspiration}

The English metric mode of *Beren and Lúthien* consists in a <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iambic_tetrameter" title="wikipedia:Iambic tetrameter">iambic tetrameter</a> (four pairs of *unstressed and stressed* syllable); in Classical Greek poetry however, the iambic tetrameter was originally 4 pairs of alternating *short and long* syllables, which is consistent to the meaning of the Sindarin term ("long-short"). This difference of terminology might account for Strider's adaptation/rendition from Sindarin (quantitative verse) to Westron (accentual-syllabic verse).

[Patrick Wynne](../P/Patrick_Wynne.md) and [Carl F. Hostetter](../C/Carl_F._Hostetter.md) explain that the metric and rhyme of the English text tries to imitate what the *ann-thennath* would have been in the original Sindarin poem.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>
