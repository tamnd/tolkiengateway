<i><b>A Gateway to Sindarin: A Grammar of an Elvish Language from J.R.R. Tolkien&#39;s</b></i> **Lord of the Rings** is a 2004 book by linguist [David Salo](/wiki/David_Salo). It reproduces all extant [Sindarin](/wiki/Sindarin) fragments from published sources, including both the easily available ones and the more obscure ones from linguistic journals such as *[Vinyar Tengwar](/wiki/Vinyar_Tengwar)* and *[Parma Eldalamberon](/wiki/Parma_Eldalamberon)*. It follows other linguistic books like [Ruth S. Noel](/wiki/Ruth_S._Noel)'s *[The Languages of Tolkien's Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Languages_of_Tolkien%27s_Middle-earth)* and [Jim Allan](/wiki/Jim_Allan)'s *[An Introduction to Elvish](/wiki/An_Introduction_to_Elvish)*, although the book's initial popularity was largely due to Salo's involvement in [*The Lord of the Rings* (film series)](</wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series)> "The Lord of the Rings (film series)").

## From the publisher {#From_the_publisher}

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p> From the 1910s to the 1970s, author and linguist <a href="/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien" title="J.R.R. Tolkien">J.R.R. Tolkien</a> worked at creating plausibly realistic languages to be used by the creatures and characters in his novels. Like his other languages, Sindarin was a new invention, not based on any existing or artificial language. By the time of his death, he had established fairly complete descriptions of two languages, the &#34;elvish&#34; tongues called <a href="/wiki/Quenya" title="Quenya">Quenya</a> and <a href="/wiki/Sindarin" title="Sindarin">Sindarin</a>. He was able to compose poetic and prose texts in both, and he also constructed a lengthy sequence of changes for both from an ancestral &#34;proto-language,&#34; comparable to the development of historical languages and capable of analysis with the techniques of historical linguistics.
</p><p>In <i>A Gateway to Sindarin</i>, David Salo has created a volume that is a serious look at an entertaining topic. Salo covers the grammar, morphology, and history of the language. Supplemental material includes a vocabulary, Sindarin names, a glossary of terms, and an annotated list of works relevant to Sindarin. What emerges is homage to Tolkien&#39;s scholarly philological efforts.
</p>
</blockquote>

## Cover design {#Cover_design}

The cover features a gateway, styled like the [Doors of Durin](/wiki/Doors_of_Durin). On the bow, Tengwar in [Beleriandic mode](/wiki/Tengwar "Tengwar") read "Annon na Edhellen", which is a Neo-Sindarin translation of the book's title. On the bottom, an altered version of [Elu Thingol's heraldic device](/wiki/File:J.R.R._Tolkien_-_Elw%C3%AB_heraldic_device.jpg "File:J.R.R. Tolkien - Elwë heraldic device.jpg") is shown.

## Critical reception {#Critical_reception}

When released, [Thorsten Renk](/wiki/Thorsten_Renk) described *A Gateway to Sindarin* as "*currently the best English book available on Sindarin*". However, he also noted that the work on the one hand might be inaccessible to learners of Sindarin (since Salo uses a very technical language), and on the other hand not very usable by linguists (because of Salo's "*lack of distinction between Tolkien-made and Salo-made historic forms*)".<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

There has been some criticism that the language described is not Sindarin, but an arbitrary fabrication of "[Neo-Sindarin](/wiki/Neo-Elvish "Neo-Elvish")". (see [reviews](#Reviews_by_other_linguists))
