<i><b>A Gateway to Sindarin: A Grammar of an Elvish Language from J.R.R. Tolkien&#39;s</b></i> **Lord of the Rings** is a 2004 book by linguist [David Salo](../D/David_Salo.md). It reproduces all extant [Sindarin](../S/Sindarin.md) fragments from published sources, including both the easily available ones and the more obscure ones from linguistic journals such as *[Vinyar Tengwar](../V/Vinyar_Tengwar.md)* and *[Parma Eldalamberon](../P/Parma_Eldalamberon.md)*. It follows other linguistic books like [Ruth S. Noel](../R/Ruth_S._Noel.md)'s *[The Languages of Tolkien's Middle-earth](../T/The_Languages_of_Tolkien's_Middle-earth.md)* and [Jim Allan](../J/Jim_Allan.md)'s *[An Introduction to Elvish](An_Introduction_to_Elvish.md)*, although the book's initial popularity was largely due to Salo's involvement in [*The Lord of the Rings* (film series)](<../T/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series).md>).

## From the publisher {#From_the_publisher}

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p> From the 1910s to the 1970s, author and linguist <a href="../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md" title="J.R.R. Tolkien">J.R.R. Tolkien</a> worked at creating plausibly realistic languages to be used by the creatures and characters in his novels. Like his other languages, Sindarin was a new invention, not based on any existing or artificial language. By the time of his death, he had established fairly complete descriptions of two languages, the &#34;elvish&#34; tongues called <a href="../Q/Quenya.md" title="Quenya">Quenya</a> and <a href="../S/Sindarin.md" title="Sindarin">Sindarin</a>. He was able to compose poetic and prose texts in both, and he also constructed a lengthy sequence of changes for both from an ancestral &#34;proto-language,&#34; comparable to the development of historical languages and capable of analysis with the techniques of historical linguistics.
</p><p>In <i>A Gateway to Sindarin</i>, David Salo has created a volume that is a serious look at an entertaining topic. Salo covers the grammar, morphology, and history of the language. Supplemental material includes a vocabulary, Sindarin names, a glossary of terms, and an annotated list of works relevant to Sindarin. What emerges is homage to Tolkien&#39;s scholarly philological efforts.
</p>
</blockquote>

## Cover design {#Cover_design}

The cover features a gateway, styled like the [Doors of Durin](../D/Doors_of_Durin.md). On the bow, Tengwar in [Beleriandic mode](../T/Tengwar.md) read "Annon na Edhellen", which is a Neo-Sindarin translation of the book's title. On the bottom, an altered version of [Elu Thingol's heraldic device](../../file/J/J.R.R._Tolkien_-_Elwë_heraldic_device.jpg.md) is shown.

## Critical reception {#Critical_reception}

When released, [Thorsten Renk](../T/Thorsten_Renk.md) described *A Gateway to Sindarin* as "*currently the best English book available on Sindarin*". However, he also noted that the work on the one hand might be inaccessible to learners of Sindarin (since Salo uses a very technical language), and on the other hand not very usable by linguists (because of Salo's "*lack of distinction between Tolkien-made and Salo-made historic forms*)".<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

There has been some criticism that the language described is not Sindarin, but an arbitrary fabrication of "[Neo-Sindarin](../N/Neo-Elvish.md)". (see [reviews](#Reviews_by_other_linguists))
