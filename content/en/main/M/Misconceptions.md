**Misconceptions** have arisen and circulated over numerous concepts within [J.R.R. Tolkien's](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien "J.R.R. Tolkien") [legendarium](/wiki/Legendarium "Legendarium") as a result of mistakes made by secondary authors and of changes made by adaptations.

## Beginning of the First Age {#Beginning_of_the_First_Age}

<div class="tg-quote-container"><div class="tg-section-divider"><span>”</span></div><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r395753">.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider{margin-top:1rem;margin-bottom:0.5rem;color:var(--tg-color-subtle);display:flex;align-items:center;justify-content:space-between}.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider:not(:empty){gap:1rem;white-space:nowrap}.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider::before,.mw-parser-output .tg-section-divider::after{content:"";height:1px;width:100%;background-color:var(--tg-border-color-base)}</style><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>The title of this second part, <i>The War of the Jewels</i>, is an expression that my father often used of the <b>last</b> six centuries of the First Age: the history of Beleriand after the return of Morgoth to Middle-earth and the coming of the Noldor, until its end.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><a href="/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien" title="Christopher Tolkien">Christopher Tolkien</a><sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup></cite></p>
</blockquote><div class="tg-section-divider"></div><link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395753"/></div>

In the [Appendix B](/wiki/Appendix_B) and the *[Quenta Silmarillion](/wiki/Quenta_Silmarillion)* [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien) never gives dates of the First Age. In works such as *[The Annals of Aman](/wiki/The_Annals_of_Aman)* and *[The Grey Annals](/wiki/The_Grey_Annals)*, Tolkien measured the First Age with the [Years of the Trees](/wiki/Years_of_the_Trees) (YT),<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup> or the [Valian Years](/wiki/Valian_Years) (VY) and then [Years of the Sun](/wiki/Years_of_the_Sun) (YS),<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup> respectively.

[Robert Foster](/wiki/Robert_Foster) among other [Tolkienists](/wiki/Tolkienist "Tolkienist") attempted to chronicle the [First Age](/wiki/First_Age); by convention these sources use the Years of the Sun as "First Age" keeping a format similar to Appendix B. For example, the [twentieth Year of the Sun](/wiki/First_Age_20 "First Age 20") is referred to as I 20 or F.A. 20, and the [Tolkien Gateway](/wiki/Tolkien_Gateway) also uses this format. It should be however pointed out that according to Foster's own remark the definition YS 1 would be more accurate than FA 1,<sup id="cite_ref-Foster_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foster-4">[4]</a></sup> and that his text itself does not clarify when exactly the First Age began.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup>

Unfortunately, this convention creates the widespread [misconception](/wiki/First_Age#Terminology "First Age") that [F.A.](/wiki/First_Age "First Age") [1](/wiki/First_Age_1 "First Age 1") was the first year of the First Age, or its beginning, marked as such by the first sunrise. This led to the [fanonical](/wiki/Fanon "Fanon") term "Ages of the Sun" and the misconception that the Ages measured from the first rising of the Sun. According to that definition, the First Age 'proper' followed the [Years of the Trees](/wiki/Years_of_the_Trees) and lasted only c. 600 years until the [beginning](/wiki/Second_Age_1 "Second Age 1") of the [Second Age](/wiki/Second_Age).

However, in the [Appendix F](/wiki/Appendix_F) to the *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)* J.R.R. Tolkien himself wrote explicitly that the [Return of the Noldor](/wiki/Return_of_the_Noldor) to [Middle-earth](/wiki/Middle-earth), which resulted from their [Exile](/wiki/Exile_of_the_Noldor "Exile of the Noldor"), took place at at the <i><b>end</b></i> of the First Age, thus meaning that the year labeled F.A. 1 was in fact placed near the end of that long Age;<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> in the same Appendix, there is also mentioned that the [Orcs](/wiki/Orcs) were first bred by [Morgoth](/wiki/Morgoth) in the Elder Days,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> i.e. in the First Age,<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup> but this happened before [Utumno](/wiki/Utumno) was broken and demolished.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup> Furthermore, in *The Annals of Aman* Tolkien mentioned that the First Age ([of the Children of Ilúvatar](/wiki/Ages_of_the_Children_of_Il%C3%BAvatar "Ages of the Children of Ilúvatar")) began when the Elves awoke at [Cuiviénen](/wiki/Cuivi%C3%A9nen) in [Y.T.](/wiki/Years_of_the_Trees "Years of the Trees") [1050](/wiki/Year_of_the_Trees_1050 "Year of the Trees 1050"),<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">[10]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup> while in the [twelfth volume](/wiki/The_Peoples_of_Middle-earth "The Peoples of Middle-earth") of *[The History of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_History_of_Middle-earth)* he also pointed out that the First Age was the <i><b>longest</b></i> (of all Ages),<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup> as well as in his [Letter 131](/wiki/Letter_131) to [Milton Waldman](/wiki/Milton_Waldman), where Tolkien described the First Age as *long*.<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">[13]</a></sup> Additionally, in *[The Nature of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Nature_of_Middle-earth)* Tolkien also described the First Age beginning with the Awakening of the Elves and ending with the Downfall of [Angband](/wiki/Angband).<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">[14]</a></sup> Therefore, all of this signifies that the term 'First Age' is required to be expanded <i><b>long before</b></i> the first rising of the Sun, and indeed Robert Foster in his *[Complete Guide of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Complete_Guide_to_Middle-earth "The Complete Guide to Middle-earth")* undoubtedly defined events of the Years of the Trees following the [Awakening of the Elves](/wiki/Awakening_of_the_Elves) as an integral part of the First Age,<sup id="cite_ref-Foster_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Foster-4">[4]</a></sup> as well as both [J.E.A. Tyler](/wiki/J.E.A._Tyler) in his *[Complete Tolkien Companion](/wiki/The_Complete_Tolkien_Companion "The Complete Tolkien Companion")* and [Paul H. Kocher](/wiki/Paul_H._Kocher) in his *[Reader's Guide to The Silmarillion](/wiki/A_Reader%27s_Guide_to_The_Silmarillion "A Reader's Guide to The Silmarillion")*.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">[15]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">[16]</a></sup> [Karen Wynn Fonstad](/wiki/Karen_Wynn_Fonstad) also attributed the events before the first rising of the Sun to the First Age in her *[Atlas of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Atlas_of_Middle-earth "The Atlas of Middle-earth")*.<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">[17]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">[note 1]</a></sup>

It is quite possible that the term "Age of the Sun" was in fact "invented" by [David Day](/wiki/David_Day),<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19">[18]</a></sup> a Canadian author whose books are widely criticized for their inaccuracies and misinterpretations of [J.R.R. Tolkien's mythology](/wiki/Legendarium "Legendarium").<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">[19]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">[20]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22">[21]</a></sup>

# Book Misconceptions {#Book_Misconceptions}

The following are misconceptions that have arisen independently of adaptations, though adaptations may contribute to furthering them.

### The Arkenstone was a Silmaril {#The_Arkenstone_was_a_Silmaril}

<dl><dd>The <a href="/wiki/Arkenstone" title="Arkenstone">Arkenstone</a> was a <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Silmaril" title="Silmaril">Silmaril</a>, probably the one thrown into a fiery pit by <a href="/wiki/Maedhros" title="Maedhros">Maedhros</a>, and found its way (geographically?) to the north, to be rediscovered by the <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Khazad" title="Khazad">Khazad</a> of <a href="/wiki/Lonely_Mountain" title="Lonely Mountain">Erebor</a>.</dd></dl>

- Cause: There are several reasons for this assumption.
    - One reason is the presence of superficial similarities between the Arkenstone and the Silmarils, as both are great luminous jewels.
    - Furthermore, one of the Silmarils was said to have been thrown into a fiery pit. It is possible to interpret the Lonely Mountain as being an inactive volcano, and the Arkenstone as being said Silmaril.
    - A final reason is the appeal of connecting two parts of the Legendarium in a way that is emotionally satisfying, as the focal point of one story becomes the focal point of another.
        - In an interesting piece of trivia, Tolkien himself may have seen some parallels between the two concepts. In a partial translation of early Silmarillion texts into Old English Tolkien used the etymologically related term 'Eorclanstanas' ('holy stones') to translate 'Silmarils' - suggesting that he may have borrowed the name and other concepts from the Silmarils in describing the Arkenstone.

- Reality: The Arkenstone is not a Silmaril. There are several reasons why this theory is incorrect.
    - First, Tolkien wrote that the two lost Silmarils would remain lost until the end of [Arda](/wiki/Arda).<sup id="cite_ref-S|Earendil_23-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-S|Earendil-23">[22]</a></sup> He also wrote in a letter to [Dick Plotz](/wiki/Dick_Plotz) on 12 January 1966 that only the one Silmaril in the ship of [Eärendil](/wiki/E%C3%A4rendil), the morning star, was now visible and that the other two Silmarils were lost, one in the depths of the sea and the other one under the earth, until the end of the world.<sup id="cite_ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24">[23]</a></sup> The [Dwarves](/wiki/Dwarves) finding one of them would contradict this.
    - Second, a Silmaril is a gemstone hallowed by [Varda](/wiki/Varda) which would not suffer the touch of [mortal](/wiki/Mortals "Mortals") or evil hands.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25">[24]</a></sup> Many mortals handled the Arkenstone without consequence, and not all had good intentions. It would have burned them and possibly burned Smaug as well if it were truly a Silmaril. Only once was a full mortal allowed to touch a Silmaril: Beren, when he cut a Silmaril from the [Iron Crown](/wiki/Iron_Crown) of Morgoth. And it was not without cost, as he would soon lose the hand that held it.<sup id="cite_ref-S|Beren_26-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-S|Beren-26">[25]</a></sup>
    - Third, it should be noted that Maedhros threw himself into a fiery pit with his Silmaril,<sup id="cite_ref-S|Earendil_23-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-S|Earendil-23">[22]</a></sup> while the Arkenstone was found deep within a mountain. Therefore, unless [Erebor](/wiki/Lonely_Mountain "Lonely Mountain") was of volcanic origin, it is practically impossible for the Arkenstone to be a Silmaril. There is no evidence to support Erebor being volcanic. In addition, while a volcano is the likeliest place for a "fiery pit" to appear, it is not explicitly stated that said pit is even near a volcano.

### Arwen, the Lastborn of the Elves {#Arwen,_the_Lastborn_of_the_Elves}

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/Arwen" title="Arwen">Arwen</a> was the last Elf born in Middle-earth.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This concept derives from being the youngest Elf whose birth is mentioned in the [Tale of Years](/wiki/Appendix_B "Appendix B"), and perhaps from publicity for the films.

- Reality: There is no indication in any of Tolkien's works that Arwen is the lastborn elf, nor any indication that Elves had stopped having children after her birth.

### Buckland Merges With the Shire {#Buckland_Merges_With_the_Shire}

<dl><dd>When King <a href="/wiki/Aragorn" title="Aragorn">Elessar</a> gave the <a href="/wiki/Westmarch" title="Westmarch">Westmarch</a> to the Shire in the <a href="/wiki/Fourth_Age" title="Fourth Age">Fourth Age</a>, <a href="/wiki/Buckland" title="Buckland">Buckland</a>, an independent sliver of land, was also given to <a href="/wiki/The_Shire" title="The Shire">the Shire</a> and was renamed &#34;Eastmarch&#34; to mirror the former.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This misconception originates from the "[Prologue](/wiki/Prologue_Concerning_Hobbits,_and_other_matters "Prologue Concerning Hobbits, and other matters")" to the *Lord of the Rings*, and is repeated in several reference books, such as [Robert Foster's](/wiki/Robert_Foster "Robert Foster") <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Complete_Guide_to_Middle-earth&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Complete Guide to Middle-earth (page does not exist)"><i>The</i> <i>Complete</i> <i>Guide to Middle-earth</i></a><sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27">[26]</a></sup> and [Karen Wynn Fonstad's](/wiki/Karen_Wynn_Fonstad "Karen Wynn Fonstad") *[The Atlas Of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Atlas_of_Middle-earth "The Atlas of Middle-earth")*.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28">[27]</a></sup>

- Reality: Tolkien's actual quote in the *Prologue* speaks about "<i>the East and West Marches: the Buckland<b>;</b> and the Westmarch added to the Shire</i>".<sup id="cite_ref-Ordering_29-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ordering-29">[28]</a></sup> The semi-colon added here in the second British edition ([1966](/wiki/1966)) serves to indicate that the "addition to the Shire" refers only to the Westmarch. This is further supported by the [Tale of Years](/wiki/Appendix_B "Appendix B"), where the creation of the Westmarch is mentioned, but not the addition of Buckland.<sup id="cite_ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30">[29]</a></sup>

### Buckland Renamed to Eastmarch {#Buckland_Renamed_to_Eastmarch}

<dl><dd>Sometimes Buckland is given the name of &#34;Eastmarch&#34; in various publications.<sup id="cite_ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31">[30]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32">[31]</a></sup></dd></dl>

- Cause: This phenomenon likely arises from the name of "Westmarch" that Tolkien gave to the part of the Shire added by King [Elessar](/wiki/Aragorn "Aragorn") in the Fourth Age.

- Reality: In the prologue of *The Lord of The Rings* Buckland is called the "East March" in the sense of its geographic location,<sup id="cite_ref-Ordering_29-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Ordering-29">[28]</a></sup> but the name "Eastmarch" is never used in the text. Furthermore, the region is typically still referred to as "Buckland" rather than "Eastmarch", and there is never an official renaming of the area.

### Déagol/Sméagol Relationship {#Déagol/Sméagol_Relationship}

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/D%C3%A9agol" title="Déagol">Déagol</a> is <a href="/wiki/Gollum" title="Gollum">Sméagol&#39;s</a> cousin.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This misconception probably dates from *The Complete Guide to Middle-earth* by Robert Foster and *[Tolkien: The Illustrated Encyclopedia](/wiki/Tolkien:_The_Illustrated_Encyclopedia)* by [David Day](/wiki/David_Day). (See: <a class="external text" href="http://greenbooks.theonering.net/questions/files/020101.html" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Green Books&#39; Q &amp; A</a>)

- Reality: Tolkien only calls him Sméagol's *friend* in *The Lord of the Rings*, though in *[The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien)* he writes that Déagol was "evidently a relative (as no doubt all the members of the small community were)" of Sméagol’s.<sup id="cite_ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33">[32]</a></sup>

### Dorwinion {#Dorwinion}

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/Dorwinion" title="Dorwinion">Dorwinion</a> was only mentioned in passing until it appeared on <a href="/wiki/Pauline_Baynes" title="Pauline Baynes">Pauline Baynes</a>&#39;  <i><a href="/wiki/A_Map_of_Middle-earth" title="A Map of Middle-earth">A Map of Middle-earth</a></i> (<a href="/wiki/1969" title="1969">1969</a>), where it was placed on the western shore of the <a href="/wiki/Sea_of_Rh%C3%BBn" title="Sea of Rhûn">Sea of Rhûn</a>. According to popular belief, its location was not decided by <a href="/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien" title="J.R.R. Tolkien">Tolkien</a>, but by Baynes at random.</dd></dl>

- Cause: As [Michael Martinez](/wiki/Michael_Martinez) notes, this is a misconception that circulates as an "urban legend" in several websites.<sup id="cite_ref-34" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-34">[33]</a></sup> As [Christopher Tolkien](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien) commented:

<dl><dd><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt"></blockquote></dd></dl>

Dorwinion is marked on the decorated map by Pauline Baynes, as a region on the North-western shores of the Sea of Rhun. It must be presumed that this, like other names on that map, **was communicated to her by my father**, but its placing seems surprising.

<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><i><a href="/wiki/The_Lays_of_Beleriand" title="The Lays of Beleriand">The Lays of Beleriand</a></i>, &#34;<a href="/wiki/The_Lay_of_the_Children_of_H%C3%BArin" title="The Lay of the Children of Húrin">The Lay of the Children of Húrin</a>&#34;</cite></p>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

- Reality: Tolkien himself decided the final location of Dorwinion, not Baynes. Evidence that Tolkien worked with Baynes on the map may be found in the transcribed map discovered in [2015](/wiki/2015).<sup id="cite_ref-35" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-35">[34]</a></sup> On said map, Dorwinion's name was written down by Tolkien and not Baynes.

<h3><span id="Gil-galad.27s_father"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Gil-galad&#39;s_father">Gil-galad&#39;s father</span></h3>

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/Fingon" title="Fingon">Fingon</a> was <a href="/wiki/Gil-galad" title="Gil-galad">Gil-galad&#39;s</a> father</dd></dl>

- Cause: This is in truth not quite a misconception so much as an editorial mistake by Christopher Tolkien. JRR Tolkien indeed once considered Gil-galad to be the son of Fingon., as in *[The Grey Annals](/wiki/The_Grey_Annals)*, and it is mentioned so in *[The Silmarillion](/wiki/The_Silmarillion)*.
- Reality: [Christopher Tolkien](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien) admitted that it was a rushed choice as in his father's final scripts *[Orodreth](/wiki/Orodreth)* was Gil-galad's father, and Fingon had no wife: even in *The Silmarillion*, Fingon was succeeded by [Turgon](/wiki/Turgon) as [High King of the Noldor](/wiki/High_King_of_the_Noldor) instead of his "son".

### Gwaihir is the Great Eagle {#Gwaihir_is_the_Great_Eagle}

<dl><dd>The (unnamed) <a href="/wiki/Lord_of_the_Eagles" title="Lord of the Eagles">Lord of the Eagles</a> from <i>The Hobbit</i> is identified in the <i>Lord of the Rings</i> as <a href="/wiki/Gwaihir" title="Gwaihir">Gwaihir</a>, as they are the same person.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This interpretation is stated by Robert Foster<sup id="cite_ref-36" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-36">[35]</a></sup> and perhaps originates from the meaning of Gwaihir's name, which translates as "Windlord".
- Reality: Tolkien never states that Gwaihir was the [Lord of the Eagles](/wiki/Lord_of_the_Eagles) and [King of All Birds](/wiki/King_of_All_Birds). Furthermore there are some problems with conflating the two characters:
    - The [Great Eagle](/wiki/Great_Eagle) of *The Hobbit* is said to wear a golden crown<sup id="cite_ref-37" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-37">[36]</a></sup> after the end of the book, but Gwaihir is not wearing one.
    - Near the end of *Lord of the Rings* Gandalf mentions that Gwaihir has carried him *twice* <sup id="cite_ref-38" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-38">[37]</a></sup> (once from [Orthanc](/wiki/Orthanc) and once from [Celebdil](/wiki/Celebdil)); if Gwaihir was the Great Eagle, that would make it at least *three* times.

### Ingwë, Finwë, Elwë, and/or Círdan awoke at Cuiviénen {#Ingwë,_Finwë,_Elwë,_and/or_Círdan_awoke_at_Cuiviénen}

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/Ingw%C3%AB" title="Ingwë">Ingwë</a>, <a href="/wiki/Finw%C3%AB" title="Finwë">Finwë</a> and <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Elw%C3%AB" title="Elwë">Elwë</a>, the <a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Three_Ambassadors&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Three Ambassadors (page does not exist)">Three Ambassadors</a> of the Elves to <a href="/wiki/Aman" title="Aman">Aman</a>, and later Kings of their people, were among the first 144 <a href="/wiki/Eru-begotten" title="Eru-begotten">Eru-begotten</a><sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39">[38]</a></sup> Elves who <a href="/wiki/Awakening_of_the_Elves" title="Awakening of the Elves">awoke</a> at Cuiviénen. <a href="/wiki/C%C3%ADrdan" title="Círdan">Círdan</a> the Shipwright is sometimes thought to be among that number as well.</dd></dl>

- Cause: The Three Ambassadors are among the first Elves named in *[The Silmarillion](/wiki/The_Silmarillion)*. Círdan is by far the oldest Elf to appear in *The Lord of the Rings*. Thingol (Elwë) makes an ambiguous reference to his origins at Cuiviénen when confronting the Dwarves over the [Nauglamir](/wiki/Nauglamir):

<dl><dd><blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt"></blockquote></dd></dl>

"How do ye of uncouth race dare to demand aught of me, Elu Thingol, Lord of Beleriand, whose life began by the waters of Cuiviénen years uncounted ere the fathers of the stunted people awoke?"

<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite><i><a href="/wiki/The_Silmarillion" title="The Silmarillion">The Silmarillion</a></i>, &#34;<a href="/wiki/Of_the_Ruin_of_Doriath" title="Of the Ruin of Doriath">Of the Ruin of Doriath</a>&#34;</cite></p>

<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r395759"/>

- Reality: *The Silmarillion* does not conclusively identify any named Elf as one of the Eru-begotten. The only Eru-begotten Elves who are named anywhere in the Legendarium are the three [Elf-fathers](/wiki/Elf-fathers), Imin, Tata, and Enel, and the [Three Elderwomen of the Elves](/wiki/Three_Elderwomen_of_the_Elves), Iminyë, Tatië, and Enelyë. The *[Grey Annals](/wiki/Grey_Annals)* state that Oromë found the Elves 500 [years](/wiki/Coranar "Coranar") after the Awakening, which leaves ample time for the births of multiple generations of Elves. We also have specific reasons for identifying each of these four Elves as having been begotten in the usual manner.
    - **Elwë**
        - The *[Last Writings](/wiki/Last_Writings)* state that Elwë was the elder brother of [Olwë](/wiki/Olw%C3%AB), and *[Unfinished Tales](/wiki/Unfinished_Tales)* includes another younger brother, [Elmo](/wiki/Elmo). Though it is possible for unbegotten persons to be brothers, as the [Ainulindalë](/wiki/Ainulindal%C3%AB) states "Manwë was the brother of Melkor in the mind of [Ilúvatar](/wiki/Il%C3%BAvatar)," it would be strange for Eru-begotten Elves who were brothers "in the mind of Ilúvatar" to be of differing ages.
        - All the Eru-begotten Elves awakened alongside their spouses. Elwë did not meet his wife [Melian](/wiki/Melian) until reaching [Beleriand](/wiki/Beleriand), so he cannot have been among the Eru-begotten.
    - **Finwë**
        - Finwë's wife [Míriel](/wiki/M%C3%ADriel) bore the mother-name "Serindë," meaning she had a mother. Therefore, neither she nor her husband could have been Eru-begotten.
    - **Ingwë**
        - If [Indis](/wiki/Indis) was the sister of Ingwë, as in the genealogy given in *[Later versions of the Story of Finwë and Míriel](/wiki/Morgoth%27s_Ring "Morgoth's Ring")*, neither can be Eru-begotten, as Indis was the second wife of Finwë.
        - However, Indis' relation to Ingwë is not consistent in all material. The genealogy given in *[The Shibboleth of Fëanor](/wiki/The_Shibboleth_of_F%C3%ABanor)* states that Indis was niece of Ingwë through her unnamed mother, his sister. In this case it is *possible* for Ingwë and his unnamed sister to have been Eru-begotten and yet siblings "in the mind of Ilúvatar," but this situation would be without precedent and unique among all known Elves.
    - **Círdan**
        - Círdan is never said to have a spouse, so he cannot be one of the Eru-begotten.
        - Círdan is said to be kin to Elwë in *The Silmarillion*. There are only two ways for Elwë, who we know to have been begotten (see above), to be kin to an Eru-begotten Elf:
        - First, for Elwë to be directly descended from Círdan. We can rule this out because Círdan is never said to have a spouse, let alone offspring.
        - Second, for Elwë to be descended from a sibling "in the mind of Ilúvatar" of Círdan. This requires the assumption of many facts not in evidence, including a spouse for Círdan.

### Middle-earth is an underground world {#Middle-earth_is_an_underground_world}

<dl><dd>Middle-earth is a world that exists inside the Earth, similar to the concept of &#34;Hollow-Earth&#34;. Tolkien&#39;s stories are a kind of <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subterranean_fiction" title="wikipedia:Subterranean fiction">Subterranean fiction</a> as they happen underground, at the center of the Hollow Earth.<sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40">[39]</a></sup></dd></dl>

- Cause: The misconception circulates mainly among the Greek audience and is explained by the Greek translation "<a class="external text" href="http://el.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%9C%CE%AD%CF%83%CE%B7_%CE%93%CE%B7" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Μέση-γη</a>". Although the translation is accurate, the stem "μέσ-" can also be understood as *inside* by the occasional reader. The notion was popularised mainly by the Greek press, especially concerned with the occult or the paranormal. Those sources leave open the possibility that Tolkien possessed some esoteric or occult knowledge, whereas the fantasy [races](/wiki/Races "Races") of Middle-earth are identified as the beings said to populate <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agartha" title="wikipedia:Agartha">Agartha</a>.
- Reality: It is made clear in the *Silmarillion* that the Earth is called *Arda* floating in space with atmospheric layers such as [Ilmen](/wiki/Ilmen), [Vaiya](/wiki/Vaiya) and [Vista](/wiki/Vista); and Middle-earth is a continent.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41">[40]</a></sup> The term Middle-earth refers to "our earth" and is explained geographically as "surrounded by the ocean"<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42">[41]</a></sup> and not being inside something.

### No Money {#No_Money}

<dl><dd>It is thought that Middle-earth is a utopia with no finance based economy.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This misconception originates from the very few times any transactions or the word "[money](/wiki/Money "Money")" are mentioned in *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)* or *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)*; most mentions of wealth are vague or in the form of [gold](/wiki/Gold "Gold") or treasures. The misconception is also mentioned as such by [Robert Foster](/wiki/Robert_Foster).<sup id="cite_ref-43" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-43">[42]</a></sup>
- Reality: In [Bree](/wiki/Bree), silver pennies are used as a form of currency.<sup id="cite_ref-44" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-44">[43]</a></sup> Furthermore, in a draft of "[The Appendix on Languages](/wiki/The_Appendix_on_Languages)" published in *[The Peoples of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_Peoples_of_Middle-earth)* some details of the coins of [Gondor](/wiki/Gondor) are given.<sup id="cite_ref-45" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-45">[44]</a></sup>

### Rings of Power for Non-Elves {#Rings_of_Power_for_Non-Elves}

<dl><dd>The Elves of <a href="/wiki/Eregion" title="Eregion">Eregion</a> made specifically <a href="/wiki/Seven_Rings" title="Seven Rings">Seven Rings</a> especially for the Dwarves, and <a href="/wiki/Nine_Rings" title="Nine Rings">Nine Rings</a> for the Men. Occasionally it is believed that each group had its own powers to be used accordingly by Elves, Dwarves and Men. The <a class="mw-redirect" href="/wiki/Ring_Verse" title="Ring Verse">Ring Verse</a> (&#34;...<b>for</b> the Elven-kings, ...<b>for</b> the Dwarf-Lords&#34;) indicates their purpose and destination  as when <a href="/wiki/Celebrimbor" title="Celebrimbor">Celebrimbor</a> himself gave <a href="/wiki/Ring_of_Thr%C3%B3r" title="Ring of Thrór">a ring</a> to King <a href="/wiki/Durin_III" title="Durin III">Durin III</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-46" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-46">[45]</a></sup></dd></dl>

- Cause: This misconception likely arises from the wording of the Ring verse, as well as the way that the Rings of Power are referred to in distinct groups (i.e. [Gandalf](/wiki/Gandalf) talking about the Seven, the [Three](/wiki/Three_Rings "Three Rings"), and the Nine at the Council of Elrond)
    - In addition, some adaptations, such as [Ralph Bakshi](/wiki/Ralph_Bakshi)'s *[Lord of the Rings](</wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1978_film)> "The Lord of the Rings (1978 film)")* and [Peter Jackson](/wiki/Peter_Jackson)'s *[The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Fellowship_of_the_Ring)* show a similar notion: in both movies the prologue shows that the Elf smiths made the Rings **for** the Elves, Dwarves and Men, before [Sauron](/wiki/Sauron) makes war to seize them. Furthermore, in Jackson's adaptation, each group has a distinct visual style (eg. the Seven have a definite "Dwarvish" design).
- Reality: Nowhere in Tolkien's books is mentioned that the Seven and the Nine were different from each others nor that they were made for the Dwarves and Men. Everything shows that the Rings were mass-produced and were designed by the Elves for themselves. In addition, the Silmarillion states that it was Sauron who distributed the Seven and the Nine, not the Elves. The Ring that Celebrimbor gave to Durin was a notable exception, being the only one of the Seven or the Nine purposely intended for a non-Elf. Only the Three Rings were made uniquely.<sup id="cite_ref-47" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-47">[46]</a></sup>

<h3><span id="Tom_Bombadil.27s_house_in_the_Old_Forest"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Tom_Bombadil&#39;s_house_in_the_Old_Forest">Tom Bombadil&#39;s house in the Old Forest</span></h3>

Various books<sup id="cite_ref-48" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-48">[47]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-49" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-49">[48]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-50" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-50">[49]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-51" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-51">[50]</a></sup> and Web sites<sup id="cite_ref-52" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-52">[51]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-53" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-53">[52]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-54" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-54">[53]</a></sup> have said that [Tom Bombadil](/wiki/Tom_Bombadil) lives in the [Old Forest](/wiki/Old_Forest).

- Cause: Not clear.

- Reality: [Frodo Baggins](/wiki/Frodo_Baggins), [Samwise Gamgee](/wiki/Samwise_Gamgee), [Peregrin Took](/wiki/Peregrin_Took), and [Meriadoc Brandybuck](/wiki/Meriadoc_Brandybuck) reached Bombadil’s house by leaving the Old Forest, which gives way abruptly to short grass, and going up a hill, down the other side, and partway up another hill.<sup id="cite_ref-55" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-55">[54]</a></sup>

# Misconceptions Caused by Adaptations {#Misconceptions_Caused_by_Adaptations}

The following are misconceptions which have arisen primarily as a result of various adaptations of Tolkien's work, such as the movies created by Peter Jackson or the many games centered around Middle-earth.

### Male Elves have short hair {#Male_Elves_have_short_hair}

<dl><dd>Female Elves have Long hair and Male Elves have shorter hair.</dd></dl>

- Cause: Elves portrayed in adaptations including Peter Jackson's movies have been depicted as having long hair. There have been some debates, however that have sparked over whether Peter Jackson's portrayal is faithful to Tolkien or his own interpretation. Some artists have also portrayed Elves with short hair, adding to the confusion of whether Elves have long or short hair.
- Reality: Even though there is a lack of clarity for all Elves having long hair there are many quotes by Tolkien implying that long hair on both male and female Elves was distinctive to Elves. There are definite examples of a few named Elves who did canonically have long hair. Both [Elwë](/wiki/Elw%C3%AB) and [Olwë](/wiki/Olw%C3%AB) had long hair, as well as [Fingon](/wiki/Fingon).<sup id="cite_ref-Shibboleth_56-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Shibboleth-56">[55]</a></sup> [Celegorm](/wiki/Celegorm) was also described as having long hair, in Quenta Silmarillion his hair is described as "golden was his long hair,"<sup id="cite_ref-57" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-57">[56]</a></sup> explicity, although his hair is not mentioned in *[Of Beren and Lúthien](/wiki/Of_Beren_and_L%C3%BAthien)*.<sup id="cite_ref-S|Beren_26-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-S|Beren-26">[25]</a></sup> There is some evidence however on Elves collectively having long hair. The [Teleri](/wiki/Teleri) Elves are explicitly described by Tolkien to have long hair, "*But most it was their wont to sail in their swift ships upon the waters of the Bay of Elvenhome, or to walk in the waves upon the shore with their long hair gleaming like foam in the light beyond the hill*." The later Quenta Silmarillion part 1. History of Middle-earth vol 10.<sup id="cite_ref-58" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-58">[57]</a></sup>
    - And regarding Finwë and the other Noldor, "Ingwë had curling golden hair. Finwë (and Míriel) had long dark hair, so had Fëanor and all the Noldor, save by intermarriage which did not often take place between clans, except among the chieftains, and then only after settlement in Aman. Only Finwë’s second son by Indis had fair hair, and this remained generally characteristic of his descendants, notably Finrod. Elwë and Olwë had very pale hair, almost white. Melian was dark, and so was [Lúthien](/wiki/L%C3%BAthien)." from *The Nature of Middle-earth* - "Hair,"<sup id="cite_ref-59" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-59">[58]</a></sup> Suggesting that the Noldor, including Finwë had long hair. There is also a note that, "all the Eldar had beautiful hair (and were especially attracted by hair of exceptional loveliness), but the Noldor were not specially remarkable in this respect, and there is no reference to Finwë as having had hair of exceptional length, abundance, or beauty beyond the measure of his people."<sup id="cite_ref-Shibboleth_56-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Shibboleth-56">[55]</a></sup> This does in fact imply that long hair was an attactive trait for Elves in particular, but the Noldor were not especially well known for it, and that Finwë's hair was no longer than other Elves.
    - [Glorfindel's](/wiki/Glorfindel "Glorfindel") hair was grabbed by a [Balrog](/wiki/Balrogs "Balrogs") when defending [Gondolin](/wiki/Gondolin). Glorfindel's hair must have been long enough for the Balrog to grab it.<sup id="cite_ref-60" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-60">[59]</a></sup> Glorfindel clearly had long golden hair, "his golden hair flowed shimmering in the wind of his speed."<sup id="cite_ref-61" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-61">[60]</a></sup>

### The Eye of Sauron {#The_Eye_of_Sauron}

<dl><dd>After being separated from the <a href="/wiki/The_One_Ring" title="The One Ring">One Ring</a>, Sauron lost his humanoid shape and turned into a great eye.</dd></dl>

- Cause: Sauron was portrayed as a giant eye wreathed in flame in the movies made by Peter Jackson. He was portrayed similarly in other adaptations such as the [2002](/wiki/2002) <i><a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Fellowship_of_the_Ring_(video_game)" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring</a></i> game for Playstation 2 and the [1980](/wiki/1980) [*The Return of the King*](</wiki/The_Return_of_the_King_(1980_film)> "The Return of the King (1980 film)") film by Rankin-Bass.
- Reality: In Letter 246 Tolkien explained that "Sauron had a humanoid form, large, but not gigantic." In *[The Two Towers](/wiki/The_Two_Towers)* Gollum comments that Sauron had only four fingers on the Black Hand, which implies that Sauron had taken a humanoid form that somehow still bore wounds he had suffered in the War of the Last Alliance.

<h3><span id="Gollum.27s_age"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Gollum&#39;s_age">Gollum&#39;s age</span></h3>

<dl><dd>Sméagol was born in the year <a href="/wiki/Third_Age" title="Third Age">T.A.</a> <a href="/wiki/Third_Age_2430" title="Third Age 2430">2430</a> of the Third Age, found the One Ring on his 33rd birthday in <a href="/wiki/Third_Age_2463" title="Third Age 2463">2463</a>, and thus was 589 years old when he died in T.A. <a href="/wiki/Third_Age_3019" title="Third Age 3019">3019</a>.</dd></dl>

- Cause: 33 is the age a Shire Hobbit becomes officially an adult. Sméagol "found" the One Ring on his birthday. The filmmakers evidently decided that the day Sméagol found the One Ring was his 33rd birthday.
- Reality: Sméagol was not a Shire Hobbit, but a [Stoor](/wiki/Stoors "Stoors"), and these had different customs - Tolkien states that the Stoors of [Rhovanion](/wiki/Rhovanion) received, and did not give gifts on their birthdays (in *[Unfinished Tales](/wiki/Unfinished_Tales)*, *[The History of Middle-earth](/wiki/The_History_of_Middle-earth)* and *[Letters](/wiki/The_Letters_of_J.R.R._Tolkien "The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien")*); and [T.A.](/wiki/Third_Age "Third Age") [2463](/wiki/Third_Age_2463 "Third Age 2463") as the date of the discovery of the One Ring is an approximate, not a precise date. Thus, there is no exact indication of Gollum's age in Tolkien's books. <a class="external text" href="http://www.theonering.net/movie/char/smeagol.html" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">See http://www.theonering.net/movie/char/smeagol.html</a>

### Hobbit feet {#Hobbit_feet}

<dl><dd>Hobbits have comparatively large, hairy feet.</dd></dl>

- Cause: Hobbit feet are portrayed as large and n several adaptations, such as illustrations by the [Brothers Hildebrandt](/wiki/Brothers_Hildebrandt) and the movies, where the feet are actually prostheses.
- Reality: Tolkien wrote: "their feet had tough leathery soles and were clad in a thick curling hair, much like the hair of their heads".<sup id="cite_ref-62" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-62">[61]</a></sup> However, Tolkien does not mention that the size of their feet is disproportionately large.

<h3><span id="Legolas.27_age"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Legolas&#39;_age">Legolas&#39; age</span></h3>

<dl><dd>Legolas is 2931 years old during the <a href="/wiki/War_of_the_Ring" title="War of the Ring">War of the Ring</a>, and thus was born in <a href="/wiki/Third_Age" title="Third Age">T.A.</a> <a href="/wiki/Third_Age_87" title="Third Age 87">87</a>.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This information also comes from film publicity and is never stated in the films or books. It may derive from the fact that *Aragorn* was born in the *year* [T.A.](/wiki/Third_Age "Third Age") [2931](/wiki/Third_Age_2931 "Third Age 2931"). The date of [T.A.](/wiki/Third_Age "Third Age") [87](/wiki/Third_Age_87 "Third Age 87") for Legolas' birth agrees with another common fan theory, namely that Legolas was born during the period of peace at the beginning of the Third Age.
- Reality: Tolkien never supplied a birthdate for Legolas.

<h3><span id="Legolas.27_hair_color"></span><span class="mw-headline" id="Legolas&#39;_hair_color">Legolas&#39; hair color</span></h3>

<dl><dd>Legolas is blond.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This is a visual tradition dating back to the works of the [Brothers Hildebrandt](/wiki/Brothers_Hildebrandt) in the 1970s and followed in both the [animated](</wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(1978_film)> "The Lord of the Rings (1978 film)") and [live action](</wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series)> "The Lord of the Rings (film series)") films.

- Reality: Tolkien never specifies Legolas' hair color (although *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)* mentions that [Thranduil](/wiki/Thranduil), Legolas' father, was blond). Legolas' hair color is one of the most enduring controversies in [Tolkien fandom](/wiki/Tolkien_fandom).

### Names of the Nazgûl {#Names_of_the_Nazgûl}

<dl><dd>The names of all nine <a href="/wiki/Nazg%C3%BBl" title="Nazgûl">Nazgûl</a> are known:  Er-Murazor (the <a href="/wiki/Witch-king" title="Witch-king">Witch-king</a> of <a href="/wiki/Angmar" title="Angmar">Angmar</a>), <a href="/wiki/Kham%C3%BBl" title="Khamûl">Khamûl</a>, Dwar of Waw, Ji Indur Dawndeath, Akhorahil, Hoarmurath, Adunaphel the Quiet (female), Ren the Unclean and Uvatha the Horseman.</dd></dl>

- Cause: The names for the eight Nazgûl other than Khamûl which some fans think were coined by Tolkien were actually invented for the [Middle-earth Role Playing](/wiki/Middle-earth_Role_Playing) game (MERP) published by [Iron Crown Enterprises](/wiki/Iron_Crown_Enterprises) (ICE).

- Reality: Tolkien only recorded the name of one Nazgûl: Khamûl, the [Black Rider](/wiki/Nazg%C3%BBl "Nazgûl") who barely missed catching the [Hobbits](/wiki/Hobbits) at [Bucklebury Ferry](/wiki/Bucklebury_Ferry).<sup id="cite_ref-63" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-63">[62]</a></sup> Although the name of the [Witch-king](/wiki/Witch-king) of [Angmar](/wiki/Angmar) was not given by Tolkien, some fans refer to him as "Angmar".

- Related misconception: one or more of the Nazgûl was a woman.

- Reality: Tolkien consistently referred to the Nine as "Men" and "kings" throughout his texts, but there is enough ambiguity in context that the point could be argued.

<dl><dd>&#34;Men&#34; as a descriptor of the human race includes women. &#34;Kings&#34; as used in the Ring-verse includes queens: &#34;three rings for the elven kings,&#34; apparently including Galadriel.</dd></dl>

<dl><dd>In <i>The Silmarillion</i>, in <i><a href="/wiki/Of_the_Rings_of_Power_and_the_Third_Age" title="Of the Rings of Power and the Third Age">Of the Rings of Power and The Third Age</a></i>, Tolkien wrote &#34;Men proved easier to ensnare. Those who used the Nine Rings became mighty in their day, kings, sorcerers and warriors of old.&#34; The preceding sentences relate to the effect of the rings on Dwarves, so &#34;Men&#34; likely means &#34;humans&#34; and not &#34;males.&#34;</dd></dl>

<dl><dd>Since <a href="/wiki/Tar-Aldarion" title="Tar-Aldarion">Tar-Aldarion</a> changed the law of succession so that daughters could become ruling queens of <a href="/wiki/N%C3%BAmenor" title="Númenor">Númenor</a>, it is possible that women in Númenor could rise to positions of power, so it cannot be excluded that a Númenórean woman could be one of the three &#34;great Lords of Númenórean race&#34; that were among the Ringwraiths.</dd></dl>

<dl><dd>Peter Jackson&#39;s film adaptation <i><a href="/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Fellowship_of_the_Ring" title="The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring">The Fellowship of the Ring</a></i> shows all nine Nazgûl as males at the time they received their <a href="/wiki/Rings_of_Power" title="Rings of Power">Rings of Power</a>.</dd></dl>

### Saruman, creator of Uruk-hai {#Saruman,_creator_of_Uruk-hai}

<dl><dd><a href="/wiki/Saruman" title="Saruman">Saruman</a> is believed to be the creator of the race of <a href="/wiki/Uruk-hai" title="Uruk-hai">Uruk-hai</a> (Uruks), the larger, stronger breed of Orcs.</dd></dl>

- Cause: The misconception originates from the fact that Saruman perhaps created a race of Orc-men or Men-Orcs in his service.<sup id="cite_ref-64" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-64">[63]</a></sup> This is visualized in [*The Lord of the Rings* (film series)](</wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_(film_series)> "The Lord of the Rings (film series)") which further established this view. However the Orc-men are not the same as the Uruk-hai, which are instead the elite fighting force used by Saruman. The movie also established wrongly that "Uruk-hai" are a stronger race of Orcs, while in reality it is simply a [Black Speech](/wiki/Black_Speech) term for the Orcs proper themselves (excluding the <a class="mw-redirect mw-disambig" href="/wiki/Snaga" title="Snaga">Snaga</a>).

- Reality: In reality, the Uruks first appeared out of [Mordor](/wiki/Mordor) in the last years of Steward [Denethor I](/wiki/Denethor_I), before [T.A.](/wiki/Third_Age "Third Age") [2475](/wiki/Third_Age_2475 "Third Age 2475") and before Saruman settles in [Orthanc](/wiki/Orthanc).<sup id="cite_ref-65" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-65">[64]</a></sup>

### Tengwar on Sting {#Tengwar_on_Sting}

<dl><dd>The <a href="/wiki/Sindarin" title="Sindarin">Sindarin</a> words &#34;<i>Maegnas aen estar nin dagnir in yngyl im&#34;</i> are engraved on the blade of <a href="/wiki/Sting" title="Sting">Sting</a>.  The words translate as &#34;Maegnas (Sharp-point) is my name, I am the spider&#39;s bane&#34;.</dd></dl>

- Cause: The origin of this inscription is the depiction in the movies, where Sting is depicted as being engraved.

- Reality: In the books, Tolkien describes Sting as a rather plain weapon with a simple leather sheath.<sup id="cite_ref-66" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-66">[65]</a></sup> Unlike [Glamdring](/wiki/Glamdring) and [Orcrist](/wiki/Orcrist), it bears no runes for [Elrond](/wiki/Elrond) to translate. It first receives a name from [Bilbo Baggins](/wiki/Bilbo_Baggins) after he uses it against the giant spiders of [Mirkwood](/wiki/Mirkwood).<sup id="cite_ref-67" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-67">[66]</a></sup>

### The War in the North {#The_War_in_the_North}

<dl><dd>The <a href="/wiki/War_of_the_Ring" title="War of the Ring">War of the Ring</a> included a &#34;War in the North&#34;, which involved fighting in <a href="/wiki/Eriador" title="Eriador">Eriador</a> and around <a href="/wiki/Rivendell" title="Rivendell">Rivendell</a>.</dd></dl>

- Cause: This has been stated as fact by the writers of the films in a DVD commentary and has formed the basis for parts of the computer games *[The Lord of the Rings: The Battle for Middle-earth II](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_The_Battle_for_Middle-earth_II)* and *[The Lord of the Rings: War in the North](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings:_War_in_the_North)*.
- Reality: Although Tolkien mentions violent incidents around [Bree](/wiki/Bree) during the War (not to mention the [Scouring of the Shire](/wiki/Scouring_of_the_Shire)),<sup id="cite_ref-68" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-68">[67]</a></sup> no extensive military campaign in Eriador is mentioned in the [Tale of Years](/wiki/Appendix_B "Appendix B") in Appendix B of *The Lord of the Rings*. Since the Tale of Years does mention the [Battle of Dale](/wiki/Battle_of_Dale) and the fighting around [Lothlórien](/wiki/Lothl%C3%B3rien), it is clear that it would also have mentioned the "War in the North" if it had been part of Tolkien's conception. Moreover, the section of Appendix A on the Dwarves includes comments by [Gandalf](/wiki/Gandalf) to the effect that extensive, destructive fighting in Eriador was averted by the death of [Smaug](/wiki/Smaug) and the Battles of [Five Armies](/wiki/Battle_of_Five_Armies "Battle of Five Armies") and of [Dale](/wiki/Dale).<sup id="cite_ref-69" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-69">[68]</a></sup>
