<i><b>Mr. Bliss</b></i> is an illustrated children's story by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien), first published in [1982](/wiki/1982). It tells the story of Mr. Bliss and his first ride in his new motor-car. Many adventures follow: encounters with bears, angry neighbours, irate shopkeepers, and assorted collisions.

The story was inspired by Tolkien's own vehicular mishaps with his first car, purchased in [1932](/wiki/1932). The bears were based on toy bears owned by Tolkien's sons. Tolkien was both author and illustrator of the book. His narrative binds the story and illustrations tightly together, as the text often comments directly on the pictures.

The first edition of the book reproduces the full manuscript, with transcription on each facing page. The [2011](/wiki/2011) edition presents the typed text with drawings integrated into it, with the entire first edition included at the back, so the book can be read both forwards and backwards.

## Background {#Background}

According to [Humphrey Carpenter](/wiki/Humphrey_Carpenter) in *[J.R.R. Tolkien: A Biography](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien:_A_Biography)*, the story was inspired by Tolkien's own driving mishaps following the purchase of his first car in [1932](/wiki/1932),<sup id="cite_ref-Bio217_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio217-1">[1]</a></sup> however Joan Tolkien ([Michael Tolkien](/wiki/Michael_Tolkien)'s wife) asserts that it was based on a toy car that was [Christopher Tolkien](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien)'s most cherished toy, with the three bears based on three teddy bears owned by Tolkien's own sons.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup> Christopher Tolkien suggests from the handwriting it is more likely to be 1930s than 1920s.<sup id="cite_ref-RG788_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RG788-3">[3]</a></sup> [Hammond](/wiki/Wayne_G._Hammond "Wayne G. Hammond") and [Scull](/wiki/Christina_Scull "Christina Scull") thought that it was likely to have been produced in a short summer break, and suggest the summers of [1929](/wiki/1929), [1930](/wiki/1930) or [1931](/wiki/1931) as the most likely candidates.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> However, in a holiday to Cornwall in 1932 Tolkien named a local character "[Gaffer Gamgee](/wiki/Gaffer_Gamgee)" which Hammond and Scull acknowledge indicates a later date.<sup id="cite_ref-RG788_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RG788-3">[3]</a></sup>

Following the success of *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)*, in 1936 Tolkien submitted *Mr. Bliss* to [Allen and Unwin](/wiki/Allen_and_Unwin) alongside *[Roverandom](/wiki/Roverandom)*, *[Farmer Giles of Ham](/wiki/Farmer_Giles_of_Ham)*, and "[The Lost Road](/wiki/The_Lost_Road)".<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup> The publishers seriously and realistically considered publication, but the cost of reproducing the images would be too expensive, so the publishers asked Tolkien to redraw them in a simpler style; although he wasn't sure he could improve it<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> and hoped the publishers could find another artist,<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> Tolkien agreed<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup> although he never found the time to do so.<sup id="cite_ref-Bio217_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio217-1">[1]</a></sup> The original manuscripts - alongside those of *The Hobbit*, *[The Lord of the Rings](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings)* and *Farmer Giles of Ham* - were sold to [Marquette University](/wiki/Marquette_University) in [1957](/wiki/1957) for £1,250.<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup> [Clyde S. Kilby](/wiki/Clyde_S._Kilby) became aware of the *Mr. Bliss* in [1964](/wiki/1964) and, once again, Tolkien and [Rayner Unwin](/wiki/Rayner_Unwin) considered publication but, again, felt that the artwork needed reproducing in order to be published.<sup id="cite_ref-RG789_10-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RG789-10">[10]</a></sup>

Following Tolkien's death in 1973, Christopher Tolkien and the [Tolkien Estate](/wiki/Tolkien_Estate) worked to release Tolkien's unpublished materials. *Mr. Bliss* was published by George Allen and Unwin in 1982 exactly as Tolkien drew, with typeset text opposite Tolkien's original drawings;<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[11]</a></sup> TolkienBooks.net suggested 50,000 copies were printed.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[12]</a></sup> A revised and redesigned edition was published in 2007 for the 25th anniversary with all the images re-scanned and presented in an enlarged format with a slipcase;<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">[13]</a></sup> in 2011 this was followed up with a conventional trade hardback.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">[14]</a></sup>

## Reception {#Reception}

When Tolkien first submitted *Mr. Bliss* for publication, Charles Furth of Allen and Unwin said it was in a class with <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland" title="wikipedia:Alice&#39;s Adventures in Wonderland">Alice&#39;s Adventures in Wonderland</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-RG788_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RG788-3">[3]</a></sup> Humphrey Carpenter said it "owes a little to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrix_Potter" title="wikipedia:Beatrix Potter">Beatrix Potter</a> (author-illustrator of <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Peter_Rabbit" title="wikipedia:The Tale of Peter Rabbit">The Tale of Peter Rabbit</a></i>) in its ironical humour and to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lear" title="wikipedia:Edward Lear">Edward Lear</a> in the style of its drawings, though Tolkien's approach is less grotesque and more delicate than Lear's."<sup id="cite_ref-Bio217_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Bio217-1">[1]</a></sup> In a review in [*Amon Hen* 59](/wiki/Amon_Hen_59 "Amon Hen 59"), Jessica Yates also compares the book to the works of Beatrix Potter: "As a children's picture book it may well be compared to Beatrix Potter's work in presentation, with its high ratio of picture to text, in its rural English setting, and mix of human and animal characters" but the role of the narrator to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling" title="wikipedia:Rudyard Kipling">Kipling</a>'s <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_So_Stories" title="wikipedia:Just So Stories">Just So Stories</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15">[15]</a></sup>

[Tom Shippey](/wiki/Tom_Shippey) also remarked on the rural setting of "a vanished England where everybody did - and spoke - exactly as they pleased, and life was consequently a series of amiable anrupt collisions" and compares it to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Grahame" title="wikipedia:Kenneth Grahame">Kenneth Grahame</a>'s <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wind_in_the_Willows" title="wikipedia:The Wind in the Willows">The Wind in the Willows</a></i>.<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16">[16]</a></sup> [Jared Lobdell](/wiki/Jared_Lobdell) also compared *Mr. Bliss* to both The Wind in the Willows - and Toad's driving a car - and suggested that "Lear's <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Nonsense" title="wikipedia:Book of Nonsense">Book of Nonsense</a></i> is among Mr. Bliss's spiritual progenitors".<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17">[17]</a></sup>

Hammond and Scull describe the work as eccentric which "defies classification" that shows Tolkien's "remarkable originality as an author";<sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18">[18]</a></sup> "it is unusual among Tolkien works in that it is a picture book for children, in which words and art are equally important, rather than a story with illustrations".<sup id="cite_ref-RG787_19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-RG787-19">[19]</a></sup> Also writing in *[Amon Hen](</wiki/Amon_Hen_(journal)> "Amon Hen (journal)")*, Joseph Houghton compared the story with *[The Father Christmas Letters](/wiki/Letters_from_Father_Christmas "Letters from Father Christmas")*, particularly the [North Polar Bear](/wiki/North_Polar_Bear) and the [goblin](/wiki/Orcs#Other_writings "Orcs") attack.<sup id="cite_ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20">[20]</a></sup>

## Adaptations {#Adaptations}

A group of Russian fans created [an animated film of "Mr. Bliss"](</wiki/Mr._Bliss_(2004)> "Mr. Bliss (2004)") making use of the original drawings by Tolkien. The film was shown at both [Tolkien Thing](/wiki/Tolkien_Thing) 2006 and [Ring\*Con 2006](/wiki/Ring*Con_2006).

As part of [Tolkien Reading Day](/wiki/Tolkien_Reading_Day) 2017, [The Tolkien Society](/wiki/The_Tolkien_Society) hosted a live reading of *Mr. Bliss* by the storyteller <a class="external text" href="http://lucywalters.uk.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Lucy Walters</a> at <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Story_Museum" title="wikipedia:The Story Museum">The Story Museum</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21">[21]</a></sup>
