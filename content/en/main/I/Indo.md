**Indo** is a polysemous word (one lexeme with multiple related senses) in [Quenya](/wiki/Quenya). It is similar to the English word "will" and carried similar meanings to "free will", "willpower", "intention", "purpose" and so on.

In one sense, indo referred to as the "innermost self" of a rational creature (i.e. [Ainur](/wiki/Ainur) and [Incarnates](/wiki/Incarnate "Incarnate")) and was taken as referring to the centre of “reason”. This could be translated as "heart" in the metaphorical sense,<sup id="cite_ref-pe17_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe17-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:155</sup> though the more direct Quenya word for metaphorical heart was <i><a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Hondo&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Hondo (page does not exist)">hondo</a></i> which itself was probably influenced by <i>*indō</i>.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>

In another sense, indo referred to the purposing faculty of the [fëar](/wiki/F%C3%ABa "Fëa") of rational beings;<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup> their "free will" encompassing decisions and efforts directed towards a fully aware purpose. For example, if an individual set out on a journey with a specific intent, that would be be considered an exercise of their free will; the specific path taken (such as avoiding a marsh or how they crossed a stream) was considered intuitive or half-conscious, as that of an [irrational animal](/wiki/Kelvar "Kelvar").<sup id="cite_ref-fate_4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fate-4">[4]</a></sup> It was through this free will that a rational being was able to use, or not use, the "chances" that were presented to them by *[umbar](</wiki/Umbar_(word)> "Umbar (word)")* ("fate").<sup id="cite_ref-fate_4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fate-4">[4]</a></sup> Other Quenya words which share this meaning are *[lēle](/wiki/L%C4%93le "Lēle")*<sup id="cite_ref-fate_4-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fate-4">[4]</a></sup> and *níra*.

In a related sense, indo was also used to refer to "resolve": the fully conscious purpose to an accomplishment of which a rational being could apply itself with vigour of mind and body.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup> In this regard, after <i><a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Sanw%C3%AB&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Sanwë (page does not exist)">sanwë</a></i> (thought), and *nāmie* (a judgment or desire), indo was the determination to carry through the subsequent actions.<sup id="cite_ref-ore_5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ore-5">[5]</a></sup> More specifically, this sense of indo could be referred to by the derived term *indonen*.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup>

In a similar sense, it further referred to the movement or activity of the fëar, and could be exhibited in the character, mood, or personality of a being. This sense could be more specifically referred to by the derived term *indóme*.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup>

Indo also included one's state-of-mind, or mood. The natural, or simple, state was "openness" (as in receptive to the minds of others) when not otherwise occupied with thinking or being turned towards Eru.<sup id="cite_ref-osanwe_6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-osanwe-6">[6]</a></sup>

While indo could be used as a synecdoche for the "mind" as a whole,<sup id="cite_ref-pe17_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe17-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:189</sup> it was distinct from <i><a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=Sanar&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="Sanar (page does not exist)">sanar</a></i> (the faculty of "thought"), and *[órë](/wiki/%C3%93r%C3%AB "Órë")* (the faculty of "moral impulse" or "premonition").<sup id="cite_ref-ore_5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-ore-5">[5]</a></sup> Like sanar and órë, indo was considered a faculty of the [fëa](/wiki/F%C3%ABa "Fëa"), rather than of the [hröa](/wiki/Hr%C3%B6a "Hröa").<sup id="cite_ref-pe17_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe17-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:189</sup>

## Etymology {#Etymology}

*Indo* may have derived from the [Primitive Quendian](/wiki/Primitive_Quendian) word **inidō**,<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:165</sup> a compound of *IN* ("mind", "inner self") + *[NID](/wiki/NID)* ("force", "thrust", "pressure" (in a given direction)) + *-ō* (genitive suffix).

It may also have derived from <i>*im-do</i> ("self, innermost being"; taken as referring to the centre of 'reason').<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup>

## Related words {#Related_words}

The basic stem NID remained in Quenya and was represented by the word *nír* which held the same meaning as NID of press, thrust, or force—all in a given direction.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:165</sup>

While indo was applied specifically to applications of mind or will of rational beings, nír could be used for any application of force, including by mind, will, or physical means, and could be applied to any subject, including inanimates.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:165</sup>

## Cognates {#Cognates}

As NID and indo had significantly different developments in [Sindarin](/wiki/Sindarin) and [Telerin](/wiki/Telerin), there is no direct equivalent to the Quenya "indo". See the [NID](/wiki/NID) article for details.

## Other versions {#Other_versions}

### History {#History}

In [Qenya](/wiki/Qenya), the word *indo* originally had nothing to do with "mind", and meant "house".<sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10">[9]</a></sup>

The meaning next shifted to "heart, mood", being derived from *IN* ("mind") + *ID* ("heart, desire, wish").<sup id="cite_ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11">[10]</a></sup>

With the introduction of *órë* ("heart (inner mind)"), *indo* took the broader role of "mind" through emendations from *īn* &gt; *inno* &gt; *indo*.<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12">[11]</a></sup>

It then became a synecdoche with the definition of "mind, region/range of thought, mood"; i.e. used either for "mind" as a whole, or for the specific faculties *of* the mind such as "thought" and "mood".<sup id="cite_ref-pe17_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe17-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:189</sup>

With the introduction of the *sanar*, which itself became a synecdoche of both "mind" and the mind's faculty of thought, *indo* narrowed to "will" (as in resolve or determination), or the internal driving force; it was then given the etymology based on NID.

### Lēle {#Lēle}

In a note dated c. 1968, Tolkien considered using *lēle* as a Quenya word representing "will". It was defined as "to will with conscious purpose, immediate or remote" and possibly derived from the [root](/wiki/Sundocarme "Sundocarme") <i>*<a class="new" href="/w/index.php?title=DEL&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="DEL (page does not exist)">DEL</a></i>,<sup id="cite_ref-fate_4-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-fate-4">[4]</a></sup> which meant "walk, go, proceed".<sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13">[12]</a></sup> The verbal forms were *lelya* (*lelinye*).

However, at some point between Jan. 12 1968 and Oct. 9 1969, Tolkien returned to the use of "indo" to represent the concept of will.<sup id="cite_ref-pe22_3-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-pe22-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:144</sup>

## Similar concept in real world {#Similar_concept_in_real_world}

In [Old Norse](/wiki/Old_Norse), the word <i><b>hugr</b></i> has a similar polysemous meaning, encompassing "mind", "will", "heart (courage)", "thought", "understanding", "reason", "desire" and more.<sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14">[13]</a></sup>
