**Clive Staples "Jack" Lewis** ([29 November](../_/29_November.md), [1898](../_/1898.md) – [22 November](../_/22_November.md), [1963](../_/1963.md)), commonly referred to as **C.S. Lewis**, was an Irish-born English writer and scholar. Lewis is known for his work on medieval literature, Christian apologetics, literary criticism, and fiction. He is best known today for his bestselling series *[The Chronicles of Narnia](../T/The_Chronicles_of_Narnia.md)*.

## Biography {#Biography}

Lewis was a close friend of [J.R.R. Tolkien](../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md). Both authors were leading figures in the English faculty at [Oxford University](../O/Oxford_University.md) and in the informal Oxford literary group known as the [Inklings](../I/Inklings.md). According to his memoir *Surprised by Joy*, Lewis had been baptised in the Church of Ireland at birth, but fell away from his faith during his adolescence. Owing to the influence of Tolkien and other friends, at about the age of 30, Lewis re-converted to [Christianity](Christianity.md), becoming "a very ordinary layman of the Church of England". His conversion had a profound effect on his work, and his wartime radio broadcasts on the subject of Christianity brought him wide acclaim.

In the 1930s Tolkien and Lewis discussed science fiction and decided to make an amateurish attempt simultaneously, on time-travel and space-travel. Tolkien's attempt at time-travel fiction was the unfinished *[The Lost Road](../T/The_Lost_Road.md)*, but Lewis's attempt at space-travel fiction evolved into his famous [Space Trilogy](../S/Space_Trilogy.md).<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

Throughout the writing of *[The Lord of the Rings](../T/The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md)* Lewis encouraged Tolkien and supported him with positive reviews, as well as enthusiastic blurbs before publication. Tolkien recognised that without Lewis he would have never finished the book.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup>

His brother was Inkling [Warren Lewis](../W/Warren_Lewis.md), who after his retirement acted as C.S. Lewis's secretary.

In later years, around [1940](../_/1940.md), Tolkien and Lewis grew distant from each other; Tolkien credited the influence of [Charles Williams](Charles_Williams.md) (whom Tolkien did not appreciate much<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>) on Lewis. They grew further apart after his "very strange" marriage to <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_Gresham" title="wikipedia:Joy Gresham">Joy Gresham</a> ([1956](../_/1956.md)<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup>). Lewis remained dear to Tolkien, and Tolkien was struck by his death,<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup> which coincided with the murder of John F. Kennedy. As Tolkien said, "The loss reached for me its climax on Nov. 22nd, not for me the day Kennedy was murdered, but the day C.S. Lewis died".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup> Tolkien also said that while he was already feeling like an old tree losing its leaves, Lewis's death was "an axe-blow near the roots". His funeral service was attended by Tolkien himself, [Christopher Tolkien](Christopher_Tolkien.md), [James Dundas-Grant](../J/James_Dundas-Grant.md), [Robert Havard](../R/Robert_Havard.md), [Owen Barfield](../O/Owen_Barfield.md) and several others.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup>
