<i><b>Roman Britain and the English Settlements</b></i> is a book by R.G. Collingwood and J.N.L. Myres. In his Preface (dated [14 January](/wiki/14_January) [1936](/wiki/1936)), Collingwood thanks his "colleague Professor [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien)" for having helped him "untiringly with problems of [Celtic](/wiki/Celtic) philology" (p. vii). Tolkien is again explicitly mentioned in a footnote:<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

<blockquote class="tg-quote noexcerpt">
<p>Let us look at the evidence. Sulis,<sup>1</sup> the goddess of the hot springs at Bath, came into her own at a very early date; her temple, with its classical architecture and very unclassical sculpture, was probably built in the Flavian period. But less than thirty miles away across the Severn, Nodens, the hunter-god of the Forest of Dean, who survived in later mythology as Nuada of the Silver Hand, king of the Tuatha dé Danann, and later still as King Lear, <i>[…]</i><br/><sup>1</sup>. She is traditionally called Sul; but Professor Tolkien points out to me that the Celtic nominative can only be Sulis, and our authority for believing that even the Romans made a nominative Sul on the analogy of their own word <i>sol</i> — perhaps meaning the same — is not good. The Celtic <i>sulis</i> may mean ‘the eye’, and this again may mean the sun.
</p>
<p class="tg-quote-footer"><cite>Chapter XVI, &#34;Religion&#34;, p. 264</cite></p>
</blockquote>
