<i><b>Roverandom</b></i> is a story by [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien), originally told in [1925](/wiki/1925). It tells of the adventures of the dog [Rover](/wiki/Rover) who is turned into a toy by the wizard [Artaxerxes](/wiki/Artaxerxes). Rover goes on adventures to the [moon](/wiki/Moon "Moon") and the bottom of the Deep Blue Sea on his quest to undo his bewitchment.

Tolkien wrote *Roverandom* for his son [Michael](/wiki/Michael_Tolkien "Michael Tolkien") to amuse him upon the loss of his favourite toy, a little leaden dog which he lost on a beach. The work is in tone a children's story, but contains many allusions and references in the manner of *[Farmer Giles of Ham](/wiki/Farmer_Giles_of_Ham)*.

It was submitted for publication in [1937](/wiki/1937) after the success of *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)*, but was not published for over sixty years, finally being released in [1998](/wiki/1998), edited by [Christina Scull](/wiki/Christina_Scull) and [Wayne G. Hammond](/wiki/Wayne_G._Hammond). Roverandom was included in the collection *[Tales from the Perilous Realm](/wiki/Tales_from_the_Perilous_Realm)* from its [2008](/wiki/2008) reprinting onwards.

## Plots and characters {#Plots_and_characters}

[Rover](/wiki/Rover), a black and white dog who lives with old lady and a cat named Tinker, is playing in the garden with his yellow ball when a wizard comes along and picks it up. When the wizard, [Artaxerxes](/wiki/Artaxerxes), refuses to give the ball back Rover bites his trousers; in retaliation, the wizard turns Rover into a small toy dog and transports him into a toy store. Placed in the window of the shop, Rover is sold for a sixpence to a woman who gives Rover to her [second son (boy Two)](/wiki/Michael_Tolkien "Michael Tolkien"). The next day, Rover is taken to the beach by boy Two but falls out of his pocket when startled by a [seagull](/wiki/Gulls "Gulls"). The boys (and [their father](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien "J.R.R. Tolkien")) search for the toy dog but they can't find him. As the tide comes in, Rover is turned back into a real dog (but not his normal size) by [Psamathos Psamathides](/wiki/Psamathos_Psamathides), an ugly sand-sorcerer who is the oldest of the Psamathists.

## Background {#Background}

In early September [1925](/wiki/1925), the Tolkien family (then [Ronald](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien "J.R.R. Tolkien"), [Edith](/wiki/Edith_Tolkien "Edith Tolkien"), [John](/wiki/John_Tolkien "John Tolkien"), [Michael](/wiki/Michael_Tolkien "Michael Tolkien"), and an one-year-old [Christopher](/wiki/Christopher_Tolkien "Christopher Tolkien")) went on holiday to the seaside resort of Filey in Yorkshire. Although Tolkien had holidayed there previously in [1922](/wiki/1922), Tolkien described Filey as "a very nasty little suburban seaside resort".<sup id="cite_ref-bio_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bio-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:146</sup> During the holiday, Michael lost his beloved black-and-white toy dog on the beach; although the family searched for it, the toy dog could not be found.

To console his son, Tolkien created the story of *Roverandom* to explain the adventures of the dog. Tolkien wrote the story down, based on his own oral version, in [1927](/wiki/1927) and also provided a number of illustrations which have since been published.<sup id="cite_ref-AI_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-2">[2]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:77–83</sup> A few years later, Tolkien submitted *Roverandom* for publication to [George Allen &amp; Unwin](/wiki/George_Allen_%26_Unwin) in [1936](/wiki/1936) and although the book was described by [Rayner Unwin](/wiki/Rayner_Unwin) as "well written and amusing" it was never considered for publication, perhaps as a result of a desire for a [sequel to *The Hobbit*](/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings "The Lord of the Rings").<sup id="cite_ref-Roverandom_3-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Roverandom-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:xvii</sup>

The popularity of *Roverandom* led to Tolkien creating other stories for his children, including *[Mr. Bliss](/wiki/Mr._Bliss)*.<sup id="cite_ref-bio_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-bio-1">[1]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:216 ff</sup> And whilst each of these tales remained distinct and separate, [Hammond](/wiki/Wayne_G._Hammond "Wayne G. Hammond") and [Scull](/wiki/Christina_Scull "Christina Scull") have noted a number of similarities and crossovers with *Roverandom*. These include points of convergence with *The Book of Lost Tales* and *[The Silmarillion](/wiki/The_Silmarillion)*<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> in both the great whale [Uin](/wiki/Uin) and the geography of "the [Shadowy Seas](/wiki/Shadowy_Seas)", "the great [Bay of Fairyland](/wiki/Bay_of_Eldamar "Bay of Eldamar") beyond the [Magic Isles](/wiki/Enchanted_Isles "Enchanted Isles")" where Roverandom then saw "in the last [West](/wiki/Aman "Aman") the [Mountains of Elvenhome](/wiki/Pel%C3%B3ri "Pelóri")".<sup id="cite_ref-Roverandom_3-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Roverandom-3">[3]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:73–4</sup> They also note the similarity of not just the spiders of the Moon and those in *[The Hobbit](/wiki/The_Hobbit)*, but that Tolkien also recycled several elements of his drawings in his illustrations of *The Hobbit*, including the dragon, the spider and the mountainous landscape.<sup id="cite_ref-AI_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-AI-2">[2]</a></sup><sup class="references" style="margin: 0;line-height: 0.4em; padding-top: 0px !important; font-size: 70%;">:81</sup>

## Reception {#Reception}

Most reviews from the general press offered modest praise, with more enthusiastic acclaim from Tolkien aficionados; reviewers particularly lauded Tolkien's descriptive ability. Tolkien scholar [David Bratman](/wiki/David_Bratman) praised *Roverandom* saying "Mum is not the word for *Roverandom*: this book can be enjoyed by anyone who loves *The Hobbit*, from the most abstruse Tolkien scholar to intelligent children of perhaps age 8 or 10." Although admitting that this was a less-polished work by Tolkien, Bratman did confirm that "Some of the best writing is in lyrical descriptions of the moonscape and seascape."<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup> Jessica Yates, writing in *Books for Keeps*, pointed out the connections between *Roverandom* and both *[The Silmarillion](/wiki/The_Silmarillion)* and other children's stories from the 1920s. Yates concluded that *Roverandom* is "a jolly good children's tale" with a "scholarly and most useful introduction".<sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup>

Writing for <i><a class="external text" href="http://www.januarymagazine.com" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">January Magazine</a></i>, David Grayson also praised the descriptions - particularly the "sense of awe" - of *Roverandom*'s world and felt this would be a good book to introduce children to Tolkien. However, Grayson also made clear that this was a "mediocre tale".<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup> Trent Walters felt that the editorial content was "tastefully done" and summarised the book: "Whether Roverandom will become a classic or not is up to the future generations of young readers and what they remember loving and what they choose to read to their own kids. But, if you're just looking for an unalloyed, unmolested good time to read aloud to your children (or your make-believe children), call up this book."<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8">[8]</a></sup>

Daniel Offer of <i><a class="external text" href="http://www.fantasybookreview.co.uk" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Fantasy Book Review</a></i> offered cautious praise of *Roverandom* giving it a 7/10 score: "While *Roverandom* will probably never be listed among the great classics, this theme rings true, making the book a valuable addition to any library." But Offer did caution that "The book is probably not so compelling that you will be unable to put it down, and it probably won’t be one that you reread every year. \[...\] The doggy protagonist seemed rather flat, and was much less interesting than the things he saw and the things he did." Offer summed up the book as "charming".<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9">[9]</a></sup>
