**Raymond Wilson Chambers** ([12 November](/wiki/12_November) [1874](/wiki/1874) – [23 April](/wiki/23_April) [1942](/wiki/1942)) was a British academic and a friend of [J.R.R. Tolkien](/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien).<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Life and works {#Life_and_works}

Chambers was born on 12 November 1874, at <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staxton" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Staxton</a> in <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Yorkshire</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">[2]</a></sup> He was an <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Catholicism" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Anglo-Catholic</a><sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup> and a profoundly religious man.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">[2]</a></sup> Since [1890](/wiki/1890) he studied in <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">University College London</a>, receiving his Bachelor's degree in [1894](/wiki/1894) and his Master's degree in [1902](/wiki/1902). He also was appointed Quain Student (in [1899](/wiki/1899)), Librarian (from [1901](/wiki/1901) to [1922](/wiki/1922)) and Assistant Professor (in [1904](/wiki/1904)) there.<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">[2]</a></sup> In 1922<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">[2]</a></sup> or [1923](/wiki/1923)<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup> he became <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quain_Professor" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Quain Professor</a> of English language and literature.

Chambers's first published work, *Widsith: A Study in Old English Heroic Legend*, appeared in [1912](/wiki/1912) and was dedicated to old Germanic heroic poetry, especially to the Old English poem <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widsith" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><i>Widsith</i></a>. In [1914](/wiki/1914) Chambers published his revision of A.J. Wyatt's edition of [*Beowulf*](</wiki/Beowulf_(poem)> "Beowulf (poem)"). Chambers continued to study this poem, and in [1921](/wiki/1921) he published his second book, *[Beowulf: An Introduction to the Study of the Poem](/wiki/Beowulf:_An_Introduction_to_the_Study_of_the_Poem). A* revised and extended second edition of this book came out in [1932](/wiki/1932).<sup id="cite_ref-:0_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-2">[2]</a></sup>

Chambers retired from the Quain Chair in [1941](/wiki/1941) due to failing health. He died on 23 April 1942 at <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Swansea</a> in <a class="external text" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Wales</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup>

## Chambers and Tolkien {#Chambers_and_Tolkien}

Tolkien likely read *Widsith: A Study in Old English Heroic Legend* as undergraduate.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup> By [28 July](/wiki/28_July) [1922](/wiki/1922), Tolkien had agreed to review Chambers's *[Beowulf: An Introduction to the Study of the Poem](/wiki/Beowulf:_An_Introduction_to_the_Study_of_the_Poem)* for G.N. Clark, editor of the *English Historical Review*.<sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup>

Friendship of Tolkien and Chambers likely started around 1922. Tolkien praised Chambers's scholarship and considered him “the greatest of living Anglo-Saxon scholars”, while Chambers also wrote in praise of Tolkien scholarship, calling Tolkien's lecture [*Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics*](/wiki/Beowulf:_The_Monsters_and_the_Critics "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics") "the ﬁnest appreciation" of the Old English poem. Chambers sent his publications to Tolkien, while Tolkien likely sent his publications to Chambers in return. Tolkien also sent Chambers some of his creative works, such as his poem *[Doworst](/wiki/Doworst)* and his drafts of the poem *[The Fall of Arthur](/wiki/The_Fall_of_Arthur).*<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup>

On [24 May](/wiki/24_May) [1932](/wiki/1932) Chambers sent to Tolkien an inscribed copy of the second edition of *Beowulf: An Introduction to the Study of the Poem.* Considering that during that time Tolkien was regularly lecturing on *Beowulf* at Oxford and working on a [series of lectures](/wiki/Beowulf_and_the_Critics "Beowulf and the Critics") on that topic, he would have read the new edition of Chambers's book as soon as possible.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup>

At that time Tolkien was also in the middle of writing [*The Hobbit*](/wiki/The_Hobbit "The Hobbit"). It is likely that Chambers's book provided some inspiration for the story, especially for the character of [Beorn](/wiki/Beorn). The book included a summary of Russian fairy-tale *Ivashko Medvedko*, that could inspire the initial name of the character, Medwed. The Anglo-Saxon word *beorn* and its possible original meaning "bear" were mentioned in this book as well.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup> In mid-August of 1937 Tolkien directed his publisher to send a copy of yet-unpublished The Hobbit to Chambers, and Chambers wrote very enthusiastically about this book. Tolkien also sent to Chambers one of his author's copies of The Hobbit after the book was published.<sup id="cite_ref-:1_1-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-1">[1]</a></sup>

Later Tolkien wrote the short story named [*Sellic Spell*](/wiki/Sellic_Spell "Sellic Spell"), in which the plot of the *Beowulf* is re-told as a fairy-tale. This story might also be inspired by the work of Chambers, who studied parallels between the *Beowulf* and various fairy-tales.<sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup>
