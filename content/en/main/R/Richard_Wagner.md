**Wilhelm Richard Wagner** ([22 May](../_/22_May.md) <a class="new" href="https://tolkiengateway.net/w/index.php?title=1813&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" title="1813 (page does not exist)">1813</a> – [13 February](../_/13_February.md) [1883](../_/1883.md)) was a German composer, poet, conductor, theatre director and essayist, primarily known for his operas or dramas.

Tolkien seems to have been well acquainted with the works of Wagner, of which the chivalric *Lohengrin* and *Parsifal* and the more Norse *[Der Ring des Nibelungen](../D/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen.md)*, were popular in England of his day. Together with his friends in the Kolbítar Club, Tolkien studied the works of Wagner during the late 1920s.<sup id="cite_ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1">[1]</a></sup>

Another member of the club, [C.S. Lewis](../C/C.S._Lewis.md), was an avid fan of Wagner, and collected recordings of Wagner, owned illustrations by <a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rackham" title="wikipedia:Arthur Rackham">Arthur Rackham</a> (a British illustrator, often depicting scenes from the works of Wagner, to whom Tolkien also warmed), dreamt about turning the *[Ring](../D/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen.md)* into prose, and took Tolkien to London to see a staging of the *Ring*.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3">[3]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4">[4]</a></sup> During the 1930s, Lewis and Tolkien apparently began working on a translation of the second, and most popular, of the four *Ring* instalments, *[Die Walküre](../D/Die_Walküre.md)*.<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6">[6]</a></sup>

In the scholarly study of [J.R.R. Tolkien](../J/J.R.R._Tolkien.md), Wagner is mostly mentioned in the context of the apparent similarities between *[Der Ring des Nibelungen](../D/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen.md)*, Wagner's tetralogy of musical dramas based on the Norse sagas and the *Nibelungenlied*, and *[The Lord of the Rings](../T/The_Lord_of_the_Rings.md), [The Hobbit](../T/The_Hobbit.md) and [The Children of Hurin](../T/The_Children_of_Húrin.md).* By and large, these similarities can be chalked up to the shared mythological sources, namely the *[Völuspá](../V/Völuspá.md)* and <i><a class="extiw" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lsunga_Saga" title="wikipedia:Völsunga Saga">Völsunga Saga</a></i> - although some scholars argue for a few more direct inspiration: In *[The History of The Hobbit](../T/The_History_of_The_Hobbit.md),* [John D. Rateliff](../J/John_D._Rateliff.md) is inclined to credit Wagner with inspiring the naming of [Mîm](../M/Mîm.md) which, although derived from *Mimir* in the sagas, only appears as the name of an untrustworthy Dwarf (substituting Regin) in Wagner's *Siegfried*.<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7">[7]</a></sup>
